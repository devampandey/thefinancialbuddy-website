Indian shares rose sharply on Friday, November 6, on a day when most of the world's major markets fell hard. The Sensex closed 1,718 points, or 2.17%, higher at 81,028.02 and the Nifty 50 gained 2.01% to 25,030.35, both ending at the day's high. In the same session, South Korea's Kospi fell 6.02% and Japan's Nikkei 225 dropped 5.96%, after the Nasdaq Composite lost 5.52% in New York overnight. A gap of roughly eight percentage points between India and its two largest Asian peers in a single day is rare, and it marks a clear change in how global investors are treating Indian equities after this week's US midterm elections.

## What is new since earlier this week

We wrote on Tuesday that India was holding up as Asia sold off its AI-linked stocks ([see our earlier report](/markets/nifty-pushes-into-the-23-500-23-600-wall-as-india-shrugs-off-asia-s-ai-sell-off)). What has changed since then is the scale. Tuesday's Kospi drop was 2.4%; Friday's was 6%. The Nasdaq's fall was more than five times as large. The tech sell-off has become a rout, and Indian shares have gained more, not less, as it deepened.

Friday was the seventh straight gain for the benchmarks, the first streak that long in about three years. Over those seven sessions the Sensex has climbed 7,782 points, or 10.6%, and the Nifty has added 2,331 points, or 10.3%. For the week, the Sensex rose 8.8% and the Nifty 8.6%. The Nifty's close is its highest in nine months; it last finished higher on February 2, 2026, at 25,088.40. The combined value of BSE-listed companies reached about Rs 503 lakh crore, roughly Rs 30 lakh crore more than seven sessions ago.

## The flows behind the move

The most striking number this week is foreign buying. Overseas institutions bought a net Rs 14,975 crore of Indian shares on Thursday, according to exchange data. That is about twice Wednesday's Rs 7,284 crore and nearly five times Monday's Rs 3,115 crore, a steep acceleration within a single week after a year of heavy foreign selling.

The rupee is both a cause and an effect of that buying. It touched 93.80 against the dollar on Friday, its strongest level in six months, up from about 94.64 earlier in the week. A firmer rupee raises the dollar return on Indian shares, which draws more foreign money in, which in turn supports the rupee. Brent crude, which fell another 1.5% to about $96.80 a barrel on Friday, adds to that by cutting India's import bill. We explained on Thursday why cheaper oil matters so much for India ([see our analysis](/markets/brent-falls-below-94-after-the-us-midterms-what-cheaper-oil-can-and-cannot-do-for)).

## Why the money chose India's banks

The Wall Street sell-off was led by technology companies, as investors questioned the returns on heavy AI and data-centre spending and weighed the prospect of tighter tech regulation under a Democratic Congress. Korea and Japan carry large index weights in chipmakers and AI hardware suppliers. India has very little of that exposure.

Money leaving crowded AI positions needs a large, liquid destination, and India's private lenders were the main beneficiaries. The BSE Private Banks index rose 2.99% on Friday, with ICICI Bank, HDFC Bank, Axis Bank and Kotak Mahindra Bank among the leading gainers. Mahindra & Mahindra was the best Sensex stock, up 6.15%, after reporting a 25% rise in September-quarter profit to Rs 4,588 crore. Power Grid was the only Sensex stock to fall.

## What this means

Our view is that this week's rally is mainly a reallocation of foreign money, not a broad change in the outlook for Indian companies. That matters for how durable it is.

The first sign is breadth. On Friday the BSE SmallCap Select index fell 0.92% and the MidCap Select rose only 0.23%. Midcaps and smallcaps also opened sharply lower on Friday morning before the large-cap buying arrived. Investors whose money sits mainly in small and mid-cap funds have seen a fraction of the 10% headline gain. Foreign funds buy the biggest, most liquid stocks first, and that is exactly where the gains are concentrated.

The second is what the flows depend on. Three things are pulling money toward India at the same time: oil below $100, a stronger rupee, and investors rotating out of AI stocks. The first two rest on a US-Iran de-escalation that markets expect but has not happened. There is still no deal, attacks on shipping near Hormuz have continued, and Washington has not ruled out new strikes. If Brent climbs back above $105, the rupee and foreign flows would likely turn together, and the gains could reverse quickly.

The third is valuation. A 10% gain in seven sessions has already priced in a lot of good news. Analysts point to 24,500-24,800 as the zone the Nifty needs to hold to keep its recent trend. A pause or pullback after a run like this would be normal and not, by itself, a warning sign.

What remains unclear is whether foreign investors are making a lasting shift toward India or simply parking money while the AI trade unwinds. If they are parking money, it could leave just as quickly once tech stocks stabilise. Watch whether daily foreign buying holds above a few thousand crore after the Diwali session, whether Brent stays below $100, whether midcaps begin to join the rally, and whether US-Iran talks produce anything concrete. A rally that spreads beyond a handful of banks would be far more convincing than one carried by them.
