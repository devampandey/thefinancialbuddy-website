Russia began three days of parliamentary voting on Friday in what is being described as the first wartime election for the State Duma since the country's full-scale invasion of Ukraine in 2022. Polling opened in Russia's Far East and will continue through Sunday, with the outcome widely seen as a foregone conclusion for President Vladimir Putin's ruling party.

## A vote shaped by nearly four years of war

United Russia, the dominant Kremlin-aligned party, is expected to retain its commanding majority in the 450-seat lower house of parliament, alongside a handful of smaller parties that have consistently backed the war effort. For the first time, the election includes residents of four Ukrainian regions that Moscow annexed after the invasion, a move Kyiv has called illegal under international law.

The vote comes at a difficult economic and political moment for the Kremlin. Ukrainian long-range drone strikes over the summer inflicted significant damage on Russian oil refineries and logistics infrastructure, triggering a fuel crisis that peaked in June, briefly eased, and then resurfaced in August. Warehouses belonging to major online retailers were also targeted, disrupting the businesses of hundreds of thousands of individual sellers.

## Dissent squeezed off the ballot

Authorities have tightened their grip on the process well before voting began. Yabloko, the only party cleared for the ballot that had openly criticised the war, was later removed by the country's Supreme Court following an appeal from a nationalist party that argued its anti-war stance amounted to questioning Russia's territorial integrity. Individual Yabloko candidates were also blocked from most single-constituency races. Party figures who spoke out against the war have faced lengthy prison sentences in the run-up to the vote, and at least one prominent Putin critic left the country last month after being designated a foreign agent.

Public approval of Putin has softened somewhat over the past year, with independent pollster Levada Center recording his rating at 76 percent in August, down from 87 percent a year earlier, though it remains high by most democratic benchmarks. Some members of Russia's economic establishment have voiced unusually blunt concerns about the war's toll, including a senior state bank economist who warned of a possible social crisis before being dismissed shortly after his comments were published.

## Why the vote still matters to the Kremlin

With the result not genuinely in doubt, analysts say the Kremlin's priority is less about the numbers and more about using the election to project unity and mobilise public backing for the war. Turnout, rather than the vote share itself, is being watched closely as the key measure of how convincingly the Kremlin can claim broad support for its actions.

The election also unfolds against a backdrop of intensified fighting, with Russia stepping up missile and drone strikes on Kyiv and other cities, and expanding its bombardment of Black Sea ports that Ukraine relies on for agricultural exports. Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy has repeatedly suggested the Kremlin could order a fresh mobilisation of troops after the election concludes, a claim Putin has dismissed. A partial mobilisation in 2022 prompted large numbers of Russians to leave the country, and military analysts say the flow of volunteer recruits has slowed even as battlefield losses continue to mount.

Voting concludes on Sunday, after which final results are expected to confirm continued dominance for the pro-Kremlin bloc in the Duma.
