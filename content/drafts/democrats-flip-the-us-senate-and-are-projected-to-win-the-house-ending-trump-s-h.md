Democrats have taken control of the US Senate in Tuesday's midterm elections, the Associated Press projected shortly before midnight Eastern time, and several major networks including NBC News, CBS News and CNN project the party has also won a majority in the House of Representatives. The AP had not yet formally called the House at last count, but the combined result would end the Republican grip on both chambers of Congress for the remaining two years of Donald Trump's presidency. Our [pre-vote explainer](/world/americans-vote-in-midterms-that-will-decide-trump-s-last-two-years-with-democrat) set out what was at stake.

## Upsets in Republican strongholds

Democrats went into the night needing a net gain of four Senate seats to overturn a 53-47 Republican majority. They cleared that bar with victories in states that rarely feature on any toss-up list.

In Texas, state legislator James Talarico beat Attorney General Ken Paxton, giving Democrats their first statewide win there since 1994. In Kansas, megachurch pastor Adam Hamilton unseated Republican Senator Roger Marshall, becoming the first Democrat elected to the Senate from the state since 1932. South Carolina, which had not sent a Democrat to the Senate since 1998, went to paediatrician Annie Andrews.

The more expected flips came too. Former governor Roy Cooper won North Carolina against Michael Whatley, Sherrod Brown returned to the Senate by defeating Ohio incumbent Jon Husted, and AP projections also gave Iowa to Democrat Josh Turek. Democrats held their own vulnerable seats, including Jon Ossoff's in Georgia. With several races still being counted, the party had secured at least 51 seats.

## Why the margin matters

Winning the Senate was widely considered the harder task. Only 35 of the 100 seats were on the ballot, and the map tilted towards Republican territory. That Democrats succeeded in deep-red states points to a broad national swing rather than a handful of close local contests.

Early exit polls cited by CBS News put Trump's approval at about 38%, which would be the lowest for a sitting president at a midterm in modern polling. Cost of living and inflation dominated voter concerns in pre-election surveys, while the conflict with Iran and aggressive immigration enforcement also weighed on Republican candidates in swing districts.

## What changes in Washington

A Democratic Congress ends the Republican trifecta of White House, Senate and House that the party won in 2024. In practical terms, Trump's legislative programme now needs Democratic votes to pass either chamber, and House committees will gain subpoena power to investigate the administration. A Democratic Senate will also control confirmation of judges and senior officials, giving it leverage over appointments for the rest of the term.

Trump's response was mixed. On social media he said he had not been on the ballot and would have won in a landslide if he had been, while in an interview with The New York Times he said he would accept the results and congratulated Democrats.

## The view from India

Indian markets opened on Wednesday with the results streaming in. Investors are watching how a divided Washington could shape policy on the West Asia conflict, crude oil prices, tariffs and spending on AI infrastructure, all of which have weighed on Indian equities this year. Any check on the administration's ability to escalate abroad or impose new trade measures would be read in Mumbai as reducing headline risk, though the White House retains wide executive powers over foreign policy and tariffs.

Final seat counts in the House may take days as close races in western states are tallied, but the direction of the night is no longer in doubt.
