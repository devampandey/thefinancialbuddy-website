The government has quietly turned India's four-year military recruitment model into something much closer to a six-year probation. On Thursday, November 5, defence officials said that every serving Agniveer who is willing to continue, physically fit and disciplined will be absorbed into the regular cadre after six years of service, with no numerical limit. The clarification came a day after the Chief of Defence Staff, Gen N S Raja Subramani, announced a two-year extension approved by the Union Cabinet chaired by Prime Minister Narendra Modi, just over three weeks before about 18,300 Agniveers from the first batch were due to leave the forces.

The officials were careful to say the Agnipath scheme has not been rolled back. In practice, its central promise and its central controversy have both changed.

## What Agnipath was, and why it was contested

The scheme, launched in June 2022, recruits young people aged 17.5 to 21 for a four-year term. At the end of that term, only up to 25% of each batch were to be retained as regular soldiers for a full career of 15 years or more. The rest would leave with a lump sum from the Seva Nidhi, a savings fund built from equal contributions by the soldier and the government, worth roughly ₹10 lakh after four years.

The government's stated goals were a younger armed forces and a larger pool of trained youth in civilian life. A widely discussed side effect was fiscal: fewer regular soldiers would eventually mean fewer pensioners, easing one of the largest recurring items in the defence budget.

Critics, including the Congress and several other opposition parties, kept returning to one question: what happens to the 75% who leave at around 25 with no pension and limited civilian prospects? With more than three lakh Agniveers now serving, that question was about to become real, starting with the first exits this month.

## What changes now

Under the revised structure, the first 25% of each batch will still be absorbed after four years. Everyone else can apply for a second engagement of two years, and those who complete it and meet the criteria will be absorbed without any cap. Officials said their seniority will be protected: they will rank immediately after their own batchmates who were made regular at four years, and will not become junior to later batches.

The second engagement also pays more. Agniveers will get a fixed monthly package of ₹43,500 in the fifth year and ₹47,500 in the sixth, plus allowances. A fresh Seva Nidhi account will be opened, with the soldier contributing ₹3.28 lakh and the government ₹7.64 lakh over the two years, which raises the government's share to 70% from 50% under the first package. According to the Indian Air Force, an Agniveer who completes six years could leave with around ₹10 lakh to ₹11 lakh tax-free from the second package alone, if they choose not to stay on.

Defence Secretary Rajesh Kumar Singh called the change a win-win and said the programme was always meant to be reviewed after four years.

## A gap between the two messages

There is a tension in how the government has described the new rules. Officials speaking on Thursday said all willing, fit and disciplined Agniveers would be absorbed. The Defence Secretary, however, described the second term as something that may be offered to those with exceptionally good performance, followed by a competitive process for permanent absorption. The armed forces have also said selection for the extension will follow objective criteria they will set.

Those two descriptions lead to very different outcomes. One is near-universal retention. The other is a second filter that could still leave a large share of each batch outside the regular cadre. Until the formal orders and eligibility rules are published, both readings remain possible.

## What this means

For the first batch of Agniveers and their families, this is clearly good news. A soldier who was facing an exit at 25 now has a realistic path to a full career with a pension, and those who still choose to leave after six years will do so with a larger savings cushion and more experience.

For the armed forces, keeping trained soldiers longer helps operational readiness. As an Air Force officer explained on Thursday, the services get to hold on to skilled manpower that has been assessed over a longer period, rather than losing it just as it becomes fully useful.

The trade-off is cost. If most Agniveers are eventually made regular, the long-term pension savings that made Agnipath attractive to the finance ministry will largely disappear. The defence pension bill will grow again, only a few years later than it would have under the old system. The scheme's goal of a younger average age will also be diluted, since most soldiers will now stay well past their mid-twenties.

Politics is also part of the picture. The timing follows youth protests over jobs and comes ahead of Assembly elections in Punjab and Uttar Pradesh, two states that send large numbers of young people into the Army. Supporters will see a responsive government; critics will see a reversal presented as an extension.

There are knock-on questions too. Several states and central armed police forces had set aside job quotas for former Agniveers. If far fewer people leave the military at four years, those quotas will matter much less, and the governments that offered them may need to revisit their recruitment plans.

What to watch next: the formal notification and the exact selection criteria for the second engagement, any defence ministry estimate of the added pension and salary burden, and whether the rules apply to the first batch in time for its exit later this month.
