A parliamentary panel has recommended that private hospitals in India's large metropolitan cities be barred from charging room rates higher than the average tariff of nearby three-star hotels, as part of a broader push to make healthcare more affordable and transparent.

## The recommendation

The Parliamentary Committee on Health and Family Welfare made the proposal in its report on the affordability and accessibility of healthcare in India. The panel noted that hospital room charges vary widely not just across cities, but even between hospitals located in the same neighbourhood, and said this inconsistency needed to be addressed urgently.

The committee suggested that a hospital's base room charge should not exceed the average room tariff of three-star hotels in the hospital's immediate vicinity, while separate charges tied to resident doctors, nursing care, consumables, meals and laundry could still be added on top of that base rate. It further recommended that this benchmark be made mandatory for private hospitals operating in large metros.

## The scale of the gap

To make its case, the committee pointed to specific examples of the disparity between hospital and hotel pricing. At a private hospital in Mumbai's Vile Parle West, a single room reportedly costs between ₹6,000 and ₹12,000 a day, compared with ₹2,500 to ₹4,500 a night at three-star hotels in the same area. In Delhi's Saket area, a comparable private hospital room costs ₹7,000 to ₹11,500 a day, against ₹2,100 to ₹3,500 at nearby three-star hotels.

The panel also cited National Sample Survey data covering January to December 2025, which showed the average cost of hospitalisation stood at ₹6,631 in government hospitals compared with ₹50,508 in private ones — a gap of roughly eight times. For childbirth specifically, average out-of-pocket spending was ₹37,630 at private facilities versus just ₹2,299 at public ones.

## Broader push for transparency

Beyond the room-rate cap, the committee's report called for a wider set of reforms aimed at improving billing transparency in private healthcare. These include fixed, unified package rates for standard medical and surgical procedures — covering surgeon fees, diagnostics, consumables and routine post-operative care — that would apply consistently across public and private institutions.

For complex or extended treatments, the panel recommended that hospitals be required to provide patients with a comprehensive, legally binding cost estimate before treatment begins, along with access to dedicated financial navigators who can help patients understand treatment costs, insurance coverage and available financial assistance.

The committee also urged the government to expand capacity in public hospitals, including adding more beds and specialist doctors, arguing that stronger public healthcare infrastructure would reduce patients' dependence on costlier private facilities and lower overall out-of-pocket healthcare spending nationwide.

## What it means going forward

If adopted, the recommendations would represent one of the more direct regulatory interventions into private hospital pricing seen in recent years, and would likely face pushback from hospital operators concerned about margins on room charges, which have traditionally been used to cross-subsidise other hospital services. The report will now need to be considered by the health ministry before any formal policy or legislative action follows.
