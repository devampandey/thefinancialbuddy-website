Serbian President Aleksandar Vucic announced on Wednesday that he has dissolved the National Assembly by decree and called early parliamentary elections for October 25, roughly 46 days away. Vucic made the announcement live on television around midday, saying he had signed the necessary decrees after the government, led by Prime Minister Djuro Macut, submitted a formal request for the assembly's dissolution earlier in the week.

Under Serbia's constitution, only the president has the authority to dissolve parliament and call early elections, and the move follows a government request rather than a parliamentary vote. The elections were not originally due until December next year, making the October date a significant acceleration of the political calendar.

## Political Backdrop

The snap vote comes after months of sustained street protests that have tested Vucic's grip on power. Demonstrations, some of the largest Serbia has seen in years, have called for greater accountability from the government and have kept pressure on Vucic's administration throughout the year. Analysts and opposition figures have suggested the early election is a calculated move by Vucic to consolidate his position while conditions remain manageable for his party, with some speculation that he may seek to move from the presidency into the role of prime minister after the vote.

Vucic's Serbian Progressive Party has dominated the country's politics for more than a decade, and the president has weathered previous rounds of unrest by turning to the ballot box to reaffirm his mandate. Calling elections earlier than legally required is a tactic he has used before, allowing his party to campaign on its own timeline rather than waiting for opposition momentum to build further.

## What Happens Next

With the decree signed, Serbia's political parties now have just over six weeks to organise campaigns, finalise candidate lists and mobilise supporters ahead of the October 25 vote. The compressed timeline is likely to favour established, well-resourced parties over smaller or newer opposition movements that have less capacity to organise quickly.

The outcome will be closely watched both within Serbia and across the region, given the country's role as a candidate for European Union membership and its balancing act between ties to Brussels, Moscow and Beijing. A strong showing for Vucic's party would likely reinforce the current governing approach, while a weaker result could embolden the protest movements that have shaped Serbian politics for much of the year. Serbia's electoral commission is expected to formally confirm the election timeline and candidate registration deadlines in the coming days.
