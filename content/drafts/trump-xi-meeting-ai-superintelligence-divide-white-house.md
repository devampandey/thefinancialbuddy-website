US President Donald Trump and Chinese President Xi Jinping put on a show of cordiality at the White House this week, exchanging warm gestures ahead of a state dinner even as their governments remain at odds over trade, Taiwan, semiconductors and, increasingly, artificial intelligence. The optics of camaraderie could not fully paper over a widening gap in how the two leaders see the technology that both now describe as central to national power.

For Trump, the framing was unambiguous: AI is a race, and the United States intends to keep winning it. Heading into the meeting, he signalled that superintelligence would dominate the conversation, and he made clear that Washington has no appetite for slowing its own pace of development. His argument has been consistent for months — that maintaining an AI lead is inseparable from maintaining American economic and geopolitical power, and that ceding ground to Beijing on frontier models or chip capacity is not an option his administration is willing to entertain.

Xi struck a different note. The Chinese leader argued that the two countries, as the world's leading AI powers, share a responsibility to keep the technology "under human control" and ensure it is developed for the benefit of humanity rather than purely for competitive advantage. He invoked the Thucydides trap — the idea that tension between an established power and a rising one can spiral into open conflict — and suggested that dialogue and crisis-management mechanisms could help Washington and Beijing avoid that outcome. He also called for tighter communication channels between the two militaries to prevent misunderstandings from escalating.

The contrast captures where each side currently sits: the US administration is focused on acceleration, while Chinese messaging leans on oversight and restraint, at least publicly. Both governments are, in practice, racing to build more capable systems, but they are choosing to frame that race very differently for their respective audiences.

The AI conversation did not happen in isolation. It came against a backdrop of continuing disputes over tariffs, export controls on advanced chips, rare-earth supplies and Taiwan — all of which remain unresolved even as a fragile trade truce between the two countries was extended by two months earlier in the week, pushing its expiry from November into the new year. Trump has previously indicated he could discuss Nvidia's advanced chip exports directly with Xi, underscoring how tightly AI policy and trade policy have become intertwined in this relationship.

The presence of Silicon Valley's biggest names at the dinner added weight to the moment. OpenAI's Sam Altman, Nvidia's Jensen Huang, Tesla and SpaceX's Elon Musk, Amazon's Jeff Bezos, Google's Sundar Pichai and Meta's Mark Zuckerberg were all present, a reminder that the companies building frontier AI systems are now unavoidably part of the geopolitical conversation between the two largest economies.

What happens next matters well beyond Washington and Beijing. The direction the two powers take on AI governance, chip access and safety cooperation will shape everything from data centre investment to semiconductor supply chains that stretch across Asia, including India's own expanding compute and manufacturing ambitions. For now, both sides have agreed to keep talking, even as they continue to build as fast as they can.
