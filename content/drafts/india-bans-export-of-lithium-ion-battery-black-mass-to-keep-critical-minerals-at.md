India has stopped the export of lithium-ion battery black mass, the crushed powder left over when spent batteries are broken down, with immediate effect. A notification from the Directorate General of Foreign Trade (DGFT) on Wednesday, November 11, 2026, moved unsorted battery waste and scrap that contains no lead, cadmium or mercury, classified under ITC (HS) code 85491400, from the "Free" export category to "Prohibited" until further orders. The decision is small in trade terms but important for strategy. Black mass is one of the few sources of lithium, cobalt and nickel that India produces itself, and until today a large part of it could be shipped to refiners abroad.

## What exactly has been banned

The order is narrow. It covers only waste and scrap of primary cells, primary batteries and electric accumulators, including spent ones, that is unsorted and free of lead, cadmium and mercury. The notification itself names this material as lithium-ion battery black mass. It does not ban exports of batteries in general, of lead-acid scrap, or of every battery-related product. Exporters who had been shipping this material freely now cannot do so at all. The government says the usual transitional rules in the Foreign Trade Policy 2023 will apply, which normally lets shipments already committed before the notification go ahead.

The legal basis is Sections 3 and 5 of the Foreign Trade (Development and Regulation) Act, 1992, the same powers the Centre uses for other export curbs, from rice to onions.

## Why black mass matters

When a used EV, phone or power-bank battery is collected, it is first discharged, dismantled and shredded. What comes out is a dark, powdery mix of cathode and anode material. That powder is the valuable part. Using hydrometallurgy, a process that dissolves the metals in acids and separates them chemically, recyclers can recover battery-grade lithium, cobalt, nickel and manganese compounds, along with graphite.

India has almost no domestic mining of lithium or cobalt and imports nearly all of the cells and precursor chemicals that go into its batteries. Every tonne of black mass that leaves the country is therefore a tonne of critical minerals that later has to be bought back, often from the same Asian refiners, in the form of finished cells or cathode material. Keeping that feedstock at home is the cheapest way to build a small domestic supply of these minerals.

## How this fits with earlier policy

The ban sits on top of several earlier moves. The Battery Waste Management Rules, 2022, made battery producers responsible for collecting and recycling a share of what they sell, under an extended producer responsibility system. The National Critical Mineral Mission, approved in early 2025, set out to secure supplies of lithium, cobalt, nickel and rare earths, and recycling is one of its stated pillars. The Union Cabinet followed that with an incentive scheme of about Rs 1,500 crore for critical mineral recycling capacity.

Those measures tried to build demand and capacity for domestic recycling. Today's notification goes after the supply side: it makes sure the raw material cannot simply flow to the highest bidder overseas while Indian plants are still ramping up.

## What this means

The clearest winners are domestic hydrometallurgical recyclers. Companies such as Attero and Lohum, and a long tail of smaller plants, have argued for some time that they compete for black mass against foreign buyers who can pay more because they operate at much larger scale. With export demand removed, more material should reach them, and at lower prices. That improves their margins and the economics of the capacity the recycling incentive is trying to fund.

The losers, at least in the short term, are collectors, dismantlers and traders who were selling black mass abroad. They now have fewer buyers and less bargaining power. If domestic refiners cannot absorb the extra volume quickly, black mass could pile up in warehouses. That is not a trivial risk: lithium-ion waste is a fire hazard, and stockpiles need careful storage. A sharp drop in what collectors earn could also weaken the informal collection chain that feeds the whole system, which would undermine the policy's goal.

There are also open questions in the wording. The ban covers "unsorted" material. It is not yet clear how customs will treat black mass that has been partly sorted or pre-processed, or intermediate products that sit between raw scrap and refined chemicals. Traders will look for that grey zone, and the DGFT may need to clarify it.

Our view is that the direction is right, but the timing is a bet. India's EV fleet is still young, so the large wave of end-of-life EV batteries is a few years away. For now, much of the feedstock comes from consumer electronics and factory scrap, which is a more manageable volume. Banning exports before that wave arrives gives recyclers time to scale and lock in feedstock. The bet pays off if refining capacity grows quickly enough. If it does not, the government may face pressure to allow exports again under licence, which is how many of India's other export curbs have ended up.

What to watch next: any DGFT clarification on sorted or processed material, whether the recycling incentive scheme speeds up disbursals now that feedstock is ring-fenced, and how listed companies with battery recycling businesses describe the impact in their next set of results.
