Kerala's tourism department wants visitors to stop simply sightseeing and start participating. Tourism Minister P C Vishnunadh unveiled a new experiential tourism initiative called "Be a Malayali for a Day" on the eve of Thiruvonam, the main day of this year's Onam festival, framing it as a shift in how the state wants to be experienced by travellers.

Rather than positioning Kerala purely through its backwaters, hill stations and beaches, the initiative invites tourists to step directly into local life during the Onam season. Visitors will be able to create an Athapookalam, the elaborate floral carpet traditionally laid out during the festival, working alongside local families and communities. They will also get the chance to learn how to prepare an authentic Onam feast, known as the Onam Sadhya, and receive hands-on introductions to traditional Kerala art forms directly from practising artists.

## From "Come to See" to "Come to Experience"

Announcing the programme, Vishnunadh said the goal was to move tourists from being passive spectators to active participants in Kerala's culture. The minister described the shift as moving from a "Come to see Keralam" approach to one built around "Come to experience Keralam," positioning cultural immersion as the state's next differentiator in an increasingly competitive domestic tourism market.

As part of the broader push, the tourism department also plans to build a distinct cultural identity for each district, spotlighting local festivals, art forms and traditions with the aim of putting them on a more global tourism calendar. Officials say they intend to scientifically document and digitally preserve the state's heritage art forms as well, so that these traditions remain accessible even as they are actively practised and shared rather than confined to museums.

## A Big Onam Week, Backed by Record Tourist Numbers

The initiative arrives alongside an expansive slate of Onam Week programming. More than 340 cultural events are planned across roughly 35 venues in Thiruvananthapuram alone, involving close to 3,000 local artists and more than 8,000 performers from across the state. The Nishagandhi festival, a centrepiece of the celebrations, will showcase traditional forms including Mudiyettu, Tholpavakoothu, Padayani and Poothanum Thira, with participation from roughly 160 artists travelling from ten states across the country.

The timing is notable given Kerala's recent tourism performance. According to figures cited earlier this year by the state's tourism department, Kerala recorded its highest-ever number of visitor arrivals in 2025, with more than 2.58 crore tourists from outside the state visiting, an increase of close to 29 lakh over the previous year. The new initiative is designed to build on that momentum by tying growth more directly to community participation and cultural preservation rather than visitor numbers alone.

Officials have framed the strategy as one intended to benefit local artists and small businesses as much as it benefits headline tourism figures, describing it as a way to generate broader economic value from the state's cultural heritage during one of its busiest travel seasons of the year.
