A criminal court in Damascus on Tuesday sentenced former Syrian president Bashar al-Assad to death in absentia, delivering the first courtroom verdict against him since he was toppled from power and fled to Russia in December 2024. The ruling marks a significant milestone for Syria's transitional authorities, who have spent the past several months prosecuting figures from the former government for crimes committed during the country's roughly 14-year civil war.

## What the court found

The court convicted Assad of premeditated murder, torture and crimes against humanity, concluding that he acted as the state's "highest decision-maker" and used government institutions to carry out the offences. Because Assad remains in exile in Russia and did not appear before the court, the sentence was handed down in absentia.

His younger brother, Maher al-Assad, who also fled to Russia, received the same death sentence for premeditated murder and torture, also delivered in absentia.

A third defendant, Atef Najib, the former head of political security in Syria's southern Deraa province, was sentenced to death as well. Unlike the Assad brothers, Najib was in custody and present in the courtroom, reportedly standing in a cage and wearing a prisoner's uniform as the verdict was read. Najib had been detained by Syrian authorities in January last year. Prosecutors linked him to the violent 2011 crackdown on protesters in Deraa that is widely regarded as the spark for the uprising which eventually escalated into full-blown civil war. His charges reportedly included the killing of children under the age of 15 and torture leading to death.

## Why the verdict matters

The case is described as the first conviction secured by Syria's transitional government against senior figures from the ousted administration, and it is expected to set a legal precedent for further prosecutions of officials accused of atrocities during the conflict. Reporting from Damascus indicated the verdict was met with a sense of relief among Syrians who have long sought accountability for abuses committed under the former government, including killings, torture and mass displacement.

Assad's whereabouts have remained in Russia since his government collapsed in late 2024 amid a rapid rebel offensive that swept through the country. Moscow has continued to host him and his immediate family, and there is no indication that Russia intends to extradite him to face the sentence handed down in Damascus. The symbolic weight of the verdict, however, is considerable: it formally brands Assad and his inner circle as convicted war criminals under Syrian law, even if enforcement of the sentence remains unlikely in the near term.

## What comes next

Syria's transitional authorities have signalled that further trials targeting other former officials are likely to follow, as the new government continues efforts to document and prosecute abuses from the civil war era. For now, the Assad verdict stands as a milestone moment in Syria's post-war reckoning, even as questions remain over whether any of those convicted will ever serve their sentences.
