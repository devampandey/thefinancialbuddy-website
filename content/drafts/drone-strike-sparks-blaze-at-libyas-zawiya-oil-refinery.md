A drone strike has triggered a massive fire at Libya's Zawiya oil refinery, one of the country's largest, in what authorities say is the third such attack on the facility's energy infrastructure within just two days, deepening concerns over the security of Libya's fragile oil sector.

## What Happened

The strike hit a gasoline storage tank at the refinery in Zawiya, a Mediterranean coastal town roughly 47 kilometres west of the capital Tripoli, late Monday. According to Libya's National Oil Corporation, the tank held an estimated 4.5 million litres of fuel when it was directly struck, triggering a blaze intense enough to cause the structure to collapse. The fire burned through the night and was reportedly still active as of Tuesday afternoon local time, with firefighting crews working to contain it. Health authorities said there were no reports of casualties from the strike.

## A Pattern of Attacks

Monday's strike was not an isolated incident. It marked the third drone attack on Zawiya's oil infrastructure in as many days, part of a wider pattern targeting a site that hosts not just the refinery but also an export terminal and a power plant, making it a critical node in Libya's energy backbone. No group has yet claimed responsibility for the strikes on Zawiya, nor for a separate car bomb attack reported in the eastern city of Benghazi around the same time, leaving the motive and the identity of those responsible unclear for now.

## Why Zawiya Matters

Libya remains one of OPEC's most volatile producers, with output regularly disrupted by the country's long-running political fragmentation between rival administrations and the armed factions aligned with them. The Zawiya refinery is among the largest operating in the country, and repeated attacks on its infrastructure raise the risk of a broader supply disruption. In response to the escalating attacks, Libya's National Oil Corporation has warned that it may be forced to halt operations at the refinery altogether if the strikes continue, a step that would further tighten an already fragile domestic fuel supply chain and could ripple into regional energy markets given Libya's role as a Mediterranean crude exporter.

## The Wider Context

The attacks come at a moment of renewed volatility in global energy markets, with crude prices already climbing this week on unrelated tensions around the Strait of Hormuz. Any sustained disruption to Libyan output would add another layer of supply-side uncertainty for global oil markets, which have shown heightened sensitivity to Middle East and North Africa security developments in recent months. For Libya itself, the strikes represent a fresh test of the fragile status quo between its competing centres of power, both of which have periodically used control over oil infrastructure and revenue as leverage in their broader political standoff.

Investigators and Libyan authorities have not indicated a timeline for establishing responsibility for the strikes, and it remains unclear whether the attacks are linked to internal Libyan rivalries or external actors. For now, the priority remains containing the blaze and assessing the extent of damage to the refinery's operational capacity.
