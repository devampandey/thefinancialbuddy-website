The United States is preparing to send American diplomats back to embassies across the Middle East that were evacuated in the run-up to and during its recent war with Iran, according to a New York Times report citing an internal State Department document. The development, reported on Tuesday, marks one of the clearest signals yet that Washington does not expect hostilities with Tehran to resume in the near term.

## What The Report Says

According to the report, the State Department has begun scaling back the emergency posture it adopted at several missions in the region and could start bringing Foreign Service officers back as early as this week. The embassies in line for staffing increases include US missions in Lebanon, Israel, Saudi Arabia, Qatar, Jordan, Oman, Iraq and Kuwait — a broad swathe of postings that were thinned out or placed under reduced operations as the conflict escalated in recent months.

Officials cited in the report indicated that staffing plans would not be uniform across the region. The embassies in Israel, Jordan and Oman are expected to return to full staffing levels, while missions in the United Arab Emirates, Saudi Arabia, Qatar and Lebanon would initially be capped at around 85 percent of normal levels. Lebanon, notably, is expected to be among the first postings to see officers return, even though staffing there would remain below pre-evacuation levels to start.

## Reading Between The Lines

Former and current officials quoted in various reports interpreted the move as a signal of cautious confidence within the administration that the acute phase of the conflict has passed. Embassy evacuations are typically among the most visible markers of an active security crisis, and reversing them is seen as a deliberate diplomatic and political statement — both to allies in the region and to Iran itself — that Washington views the situation as stabilising.

The move comes even as tensions with Tehran remain far from resolved on other fronts. In recent days, US officials including Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent and Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth have taken a hard line publicly, unveiling a broad sanctions push aimed at cutting off Iran's remaining trade in oil, shipping and other sectors, while not ruling out further military action if provoked. The return of diplomatic staff, therefore, appears to reflect a narrower judgment about the physical security risk to US personnel in the region rather than a broader diplomatic thaw with Iran.

## Why It Matters

For countries and businesses across the Gulf and wider Middle East, a fuller return of US diplomatic staffing is often read as a proxy indicator of risk perception — affecting everything from insurance premiums on shipping through the Strait of Hormuz to investor sentiment in regional markets. Embassies play a central role in visa processing, trade facilitation and security coordination, so extended skeleton staffing has had knock-on effects for business travel and diplomatic engagement throughout the evacuation period.

The State Department has not issued a formal public statement confirming the timeline, and the report describes the plans as still developing. Further details are expected in the coming days as individual missions confirm staffing schedules.
