King Harald V of Norway, one of Europe's longest-serving and most popular monarchs, died early Friday morning at an Oslo hospital at the age of 89, the Royal Palace announced, bringing to a close a 35-year reign that spanned Norway's transformation into a modern, oil-rich Nordic democracy.

The palace said Harald passed away peacefully in the presence of his family after being admitted roughly ten days earlier for complications from haemolytic anaemia, a rare blood disorder that depletes red blood cells and causes severe fatigue. His condition had worsened sharply in the preceding days, prompting his family to gather at his bedside. The royal flag was lowered to half-mast above the palace in Oslo following his death, and crowds gathered in the surrounding square to lay flowers.

## A reign that modernised the monarchy

Harald leaves behind his wife of nearly six decades, Queen Sonja, their daughter Princess Martha Louise, and six grandchildren. His son, Crown Prince Haakon, 53, has now ascended to the throne as King Haakon VIII and was scheduled to convene an extraordinary council of state with the Norwegian government on the day of his father's death, a formal step marking the transfer of the crown.

Born in 1937, Harald's early life was shaped by the turmoil of the Second World War. When Nazi Germany occupied Norway in 1940, his mother took him and his siblings first to Sweden and then, at the invitation of US President Franklin D. Roosevelt, to Washington, where the family lived until returning to Norway in 1945. He later studied social science, history and economics at Balliol College, Oxford, after serving in the Norwegian military as crown prince.

Harald was known for breaking with royal convention in 1968 when he married Sonja Haraldsen, the daughter of a businessman, over the initial objections of his father, King Olav V, who favoured a match with a fellow European royal family. The marriage became one of the defining, and eventually celebrated, features of his public life. He became regent in 1990 when Olav fell ill and formally ascended the throne after his father's death in January 1991.

## International tributes

Tributes arrived quickly from foreign heads of state. Britain's King Charles III, a cousin of Harald and godfather to King Haakon, said in a statement that he and Queen Camilla were "profoundly sad" at the news and extended condolences to Queen Sonja and the royal family, describing the historically close ties between the British and Norwegian royal houses, including their shared experience during the Second World War.

## Why the succession matters

While Norway's monarchy is largely ceremonial, the transition carries symbolic and political weight for a country that has used its royal family as a stabilising, unifying institution through decades of social and economic change, including its emergence as a major oil and gas exporter. King Haakon VIII inherits a monarchy widely regarded as one of the most popular in Europe, built in large part on his father's reputation for accessibility and his efforts to keep the institution closely connected to ordinary Norwegians. How the new king balances continuity with his own approach to the role will be closely watched both within Norway and among the other European monarchies with which the Norwegian crown maintains close family and diplomatic ties.
