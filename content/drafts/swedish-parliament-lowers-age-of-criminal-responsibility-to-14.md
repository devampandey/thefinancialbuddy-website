Sweden's parliament voted on Thursday to lower the age of criminal responsibility from 15 to 14, a major shift in the country's youth justice framework that arrives just weeks before a closely watched general election.

## How the vote went

Lawmakers backed the change by a wide margin, with 281 voting in favour and 66 against. Notably, the opposition Social Democrats joined the governing coalition in supporting the measure, suggesting a rare moment of cross-party consensus on an issue that has dominated Swedish politics for years: youth-linked crime.

The government, led by Prime Minister Ulf Kristersson's minority conservative coalition and backed in parliament by the far-right Sweden Democrats, had originally pushed to lower the threshold further, to 13. That proposal was withdrawn in June after it became clear it could not secure majority support. A government-appointed commissioner had recommended 14 as the appropriate age, and the bulk of roughly 126 groups consulted on the steeper proposal, including police and prison service representatives, had opposed dropping it to 13.

## Why now

Sweden has spent more than a decade grappling with gang violence tied largely to competition over the illegal drug trade, with young people increasingly recruited into criminal networks partly because Swedish law has historically shielded minors from the harshest penalties. Kristersson's government made cracking down on this violence a central plank of its platform after taking office in 2022, and the criminal responsibility age has become a flashpoint in that broader law-and-order push.

Alongside lowering the threshold, parliament also voted to reduce the discount young offenders receive on prison sentences. Under the revised rules, offenders under 18 can now be jailed for terms of up to 18 years, a sharp departure from the more lenient sentencing framework that previously applied to minors.

## Timing and political stakes

The new law is set to take effect on September 10, just three days before Sweden holds its general election, ensuring the issue stays squarely in the public conversation through the final stretch of campaigning. Polling has shown the race tightening: a recent Demoskop survey for the newspaper Svenska Dagbladet put the governing coalition and its far-right backers at 47 percent support against 51 percent for the centre-left opposition bloc, a gap that has been narrowing in recent weeks.

The reform is likely to be viewed by supporters as a necessary response to years of escalating gang violence and by critics as a punitive step that risks funnelling more teenagers into the criminal justice system. Given the timing, both sides are expected to make the issue central to their closing campaign pitches, with the law's real-world impact on youth crime unlikely to be visible before voters head to the polls.
