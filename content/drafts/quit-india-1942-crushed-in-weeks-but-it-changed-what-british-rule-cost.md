On the evening of 8 August 1942, the All India Congress Committee passed the Quit India resolution at Gowalia Tank Maidan in Bombay. Gandhi told the crowd the choice was now "do or die". Before sunrise on 9 August he was under arrest, along with Nehru, Patel, Azad and the rest of the Working Committee. Congress was banned, its offices were sealed, and newspapers were barred from printing his speech.

None of this was improvised. Letters between London and Delhi from July 1942 show the government expected the resolution and had settled on its response: arrest the leaders, close the party, control the press. A movement with no leaders, no funds and no newspapers should have ended within a week. It did not, and the reasons say more about the movement than the speech does.

## What filled the gap

Aruna Asaf Ali hoisted the flag at Gowalia Tank on 9 August and then went underground. On 14 August a group around 22-year-old Usha Mehta began broadcasting Congress Radio from a hidden transmitter, and stayed on air for about three months until her arrest. In Midnapore, 72-year-old Matangini Hazra was shot dead on 29 September leading a march on the Tamluk police station. Jayaprakash Narayan broke out of Hazaribagh jail in November.

Beyond the famous names, mill workers in Bombay, Ahmedabad and Jamshedpur stayed away for weeks, students struck, and rail lines, telegraph wires and police posts were attacked in Bihar and the United Provinces. On 31 August the Viceroy, Lord Linlithgow, wrote to Churchill that he faced the most serious rebellion since 1857.

Around 100,000 people were arrested. Deaths are disputed: official British figures say roughly a thousand, while nationalist accounts at the time claimed far more. Historians generally treat the official number as a floor.

Leaderless also meant unrestrained. Gandhi had called off movements in 1922 and 1934 when violence broke out. In 1942 he was in prison and could not, and the sabotage that followed is part of why the movement is still argued over.

## Who stayed out

Not every large organisation joined. The Muslim League under Jinnah opposed the movement, fearing a British exit would hand power to a Congress-led majority before its own demand was settled. The Communist Party stayed away after the Soviet Union entered the war. Other groups chose cooperation with the government or kept clear of the agitation.

Each had its reasons: wartime calculation, fear of a communal outcome, or a bet that cooperation would earn influence. They were political choices with consequences, and the record should be read as it is.

## What it actually won

The common claim is that Quit India won freedom. The evidence does not support that. By early 1943 the movement was suppressed, the leaders were still in jail, and Britain had not left.

What changed was the basis of the argument. After 1942, no serious official could say India was held with the consent of the governed. Holding it meant a large garrison and a readiness to jail the whole political class, while the war drained Britain's finances. The events that ended the Raj came later: Labour's victory in July 1945 on a promise of colonial freedom, the INA trials, the naval mutiny of 1946, and an empty treasury. Quit India is why those events landed the way they did.

## Our conclusion

When the top of the movement was removed, local networks of workers, students and small-town activists kept going, which says what two decades of organising had built. And the dispute over who took part is mostly a dispute about moral standing today. The more accurate reading is narrower: the movement was incomplete, partly violent, suppressed on its own terms, and still decisive for what it forced Britain to admit.
