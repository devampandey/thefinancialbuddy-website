The United States has moved its economic campaign against Iran from the oil fields to the factory floor. On Thursday the Treasury Department imposed sanctions on Iran's two largest carmakers, its national railway and a cluster of steel and heavy-machinery companies, describing these sectors as among the last major sources of revenue and logistics still available to Tehran.

The measures fall under Operation Economic Outcast, the administration's effort to choke off the Iranian government's income while the war between the two countries remains unresolved.

## Who was targeted

The headline names are Iran Khodro (IKCO) and SAIPA, which between them dominate the domestic car market and also export to neighbouring countries. In total, seven Iranian automakers were sanctioned, along with Niroo Motor, the country's largest motorcycle manufacturer.

On the rail side, the list includes the state-owned Islamic Republic of Iran Railway Company, passenger operator Raja Passenger Trains Company and a private freight carrier.

Industrial firms were also hit. The Treasury named Heavy Equipment Production Company (HEPCO), which it described as one of the Middle East's biggest makers of mining and road-building machinery, and HEPCO's subsidiary in Shanghai. A steel and oil export network run by two Hong Kong-based businessmen holding Iranian and Dominican citizenship was sanctioned too, along with two China-based steel companies said to have acted on that network's behalf.

## The foreign supply chain

The action reaches well beyond Iran. Indonesia's Golden Motorcycle Company and auto-parts suppliers in the United Arab Emirates, Turkey and Hong Kong were designated for allegedly helping Iran's vehicle industry keep running despite earlier restrictions. Firms in the UAE and Germany were named for supporting Iranian steelmakers.

According to CBS News, US officials see China as a key route for car parts reaching Iran. Syria and Iraq, both large buyers of Iranian-built cars, are being kept informed in an effort to limit fallout for their own markets.

## Why autos and rail, and why now

Treasury officials believe the US naval blockade has already cut deeply into Iran's oil earnings, CBS reported, freeing them to go after other income streams. Iran's car and rail sectors are closely tied to the Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps, the Treasury said, and this round is not expected to be the last.

The timing matters diplomatically. Iranian officials said this week that Washington had replied to Tehran's proposal for a seven-day ceasefire and a phased reopening of the Strait of Hormuz with a counter-offer that Iran is studying. At the same time, President Donald Trump told TIME magazine that US military action could resume after the November midterm elections if no acceptable deal is reached. Thursday's sanctions add economic pressure while those talks drag on.

## What it means beyond the region

The direct trade impact outside the Middle East is limited, since Iranian cars and rolling stock are sold mainly at home and in neighbouring states. The wider significance lies in the secondary-sanctions risk. Any company, bank or shipper that deals with the designated entities can itself be cut off from the US financial system.

That risk is familiar to Indian firms. In August, the Treasury sanctioned [four India-based companies over Iranian petroleum trade](/business/us-sanctions-four-indian-firms-over-119-million-iranian-oil-trade) under the same programme. With the net now covering metals, machinery and vehicle components, exporters and logistics firms trading with Gulf intermediaries will need to check counterparties more carefully.

Tehran has repeatedly called US sanctions economic warfare. Iran's president said on Thursday that the country remains open to dialogue but will not bow to pressure.
