American economists Robert E. Hall and Michael Woodford have been awarded the 2026 Sveriges Riksbank Prize in Economic Sciences in Memory of Alfred Nobel. The Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences announced the award in Stockholm on Monday, citing the pair "for microeconomic foundations of macroeconomic stabilisation".

The economics award was the last of this year's six Nobel announcements. The prizes for medicine, physics, chemistry, literature and peace were all named last week. This year's economics prize is worth about $1.2 million, which the two laureates will share.

## What the prize recognises

Macroeconomics asks big questions. Why do economies go into recession? How much can a central bank or a government do to soften the blow? For decades, many of the models used to answer those questions were built from broad totals like national output, total spending and the overall price level. They said little about the households and firms whose choices actually add up to those numbers.

Hall and Woodford are among the economists who helped close that gap. Their work starts from how individual people and companies make decisions, such as how much to spend or save, when to hire, and when to change prices. It then builds up from those decisions to explain how the whole economy behaves, and what stabilisation policy can and cannot do.

That approach is now standard in central banks. When policymakers model how a rate change will affect inflation and jobs, they usually rely on frameworks that trace through how households and firms are likely to react, rather than on fixed relationships between totals.

## Who the laureates are

Hall, a longtime economist at Stanford University, is known for research showing that household consumption is much harder to predict from past income than older models assumed. That finding pushed the field to take seriously how people plan their spending around expected future income. He has also written widely on unemployment and how labour markets adjust over the business cycle, and spent many years leading the panel of US economists that formally dates American recessions.

Woodford, a professor at Columbia University, is best known for his 2003 book *Interest and Prices*. It became a core text for what economists call the New Keynesian approach to monetary policy. His work stresses that expectations matter enormously: what a central bank signals about its future path for interest rates can shape the economy as much as the rate it sets today. That thinking underpins the "forward guidance" that major central banks now use routinely.

## Why it matters now

The award lands at a time when central banks around the world are again juggling inflation, slower growth and energy price shocks. In India, the Reserve Bank last week [raised its repo rate to 5.5% in its first hike since 2023](/business/rbi-raises-repo-rate-to-5-5-in-first-hike-since-2023-shifts-to-calibrated-tighten), and its messaging about how far it will go next is being watched as closely as the hike itself. That focus on communication and expectations is the kind of policy question Woodford's research helped define.

The economics prize was not part of Alfred Nobel's original will. Sweden's central bank set it up in 1969, and it has been awarded alongside the other Nobel prizes ever since. Most of its winners have been American. Only three women have received it, the latest being Claudia Goldin in 2023.

Last year's prize went to Joel Mokyr, Philippe Aghion and Peter Howitt for their work on how innovation and "creative destruction" drive long-term growth.
