Brazil's official presidential campaign season began on Sunday, setting up a closely watched contest between leftist incumbent Luiz Inacio Lula da Silva and right-wing challenger Flavio Bolsonaro ahead of the country's October 4 election.

## Campaigning Begins in Earnest

With candidate registration deadlines now behind them, Brazilian parties and candidates were cleared to formally start street and digital campaigning on Sunday, kicking off a roughly seven-week sprint to the first round of voting. Under Brazil's election rules, candidates can now advertise publicly and online, though robocalls and bulk text messages sent without a recipient's consent remain prohibited.

Lula marked the occasion with a rally in Sao Bernardo do Campo, in Sao Paulo state — a symbolically important location where he first built his political career decades ago as a metalworker and trade unionist. The setting was widely seen as an attempt to reconnect with the working-class base that propelled his earlier rise to the presidency, as he seeks what would be a fourth term in office.

## A Tight, Polarised Race

Flavio Bolsonaro, a senator and son of former president Jair Bolsonaro, is running as the standard-bearer for Brazil's right, having earlier named conservative congressman Alfredo Gaspar as his running mate. Recent polling from the Quaest institute, released just before campaigning began, showed a near-statistical dead heat in a simulated runoff: Lula at 43 percent to Bolsonaro's 40 percent, within the survey's margin of error.

Notably, both frontrunners are said to carry rejection rates above 50 percent among the electorate, meaning the race may ultimately be decided by a relatively narrow slice of undecided and independent voters — a group where analysts say Bolsonaro has been making gains in recent weeks.

## The Trump Factor

The campaign is unfolding against the backdrop of active involvement from Washington. United States President Donald Trump has been vocal in backing conservative candidates across Latin America in recent election cycles, and his administration's posture toward Brazil is expected to remain a live issue through the campaign, particularly on trade and sovereignty questions that Lula has already sought to place at the center of his re-election pitch.

## What Comes Next

Brazilians go to the polls for the first round on October 4. If no candidate secures a majority, the top two finishers will proceed to a runoff on October 25. With both leading camps facing high disapproval numbers and the electorate described by observers as weary of familiar political faces, the coming weeks of campaigning are expected to focus heavily on the economy, public safety and Brazil's positioning amid rising global trade tensions.

The outcome will be closely watched well beyond Brazil's borders, given the country's role as Latin America's largest economy and a key voice in global forums on trade and climate policy.
