Yemen's Iran-aligned Houthi movement seized control of the strategic Red Sea port city of Mocha on Thursday, according to multiple Yemeni government military sources, marking one of the most significant territorial gains of a renewed western-coast offensive and bringing the group closer to dominating one of the world's most important shipping corridors.

Government sources told international wire services that Houthi fighters entered Mocha and took the coastal city after weeks of escalating missile, drone and explosive-laden boat attacks that had already forced the port to suspend commercial and maritime operations in mid-August. Local reporting described a large-scale exodus of residents fleeing south towards Aden as the takeover unfolded, while government-aligned forces regrouped further down the coast near Dhubab, a town that sits directly opposite the island of Perim on the Bab el-Mandeb Strait.

## Why Mocha Matters

Bab el-Mandeb, sometimes called the "Gate of Tears" for its treacherous currents, is the narrow waterway separating Yemen from Djibouti and Eritrea at the southern mouth of the Red Sea. It is one of the busiest maritime chokepoints on earth, carrying a substantial share of global container traffic and petroleum shipments between Asia, the Gulf and Europe via the Suez Canal. Control of Mocha, and the islands and coastal towns around it, gives the Houthis significantly greater leverage over vessels transiting the strait at a moment when Middle East shipping routes are already under severe strain.

Government forces loyal to Yemen's internationally recognised administration have suffered a string of setbacks in recent days. Tareq Saleh, deputy chairman of Yemen's Presidential Leadership Council, said his western-coast forces had taken heavy losses and had been ordered to regroup and set up an alternative command centre in coordination with the Saudi-led coalition. Military sources also reported a withdrawal overnight from the Hays district in southern Hodeidah, part of a broader repositioning along the coast as Houthi forces press their advantage in Hodeidah and Taiz provinces.

## Part Of A Wider Regional Escalation

The fighting in Yemen has intensified alongside the broader confrontation between Iran and the United States, which has continued for months and has already drawn in Gulf shipping lanes further north through the Strait of Hormuz. Yemeni officials have accused Tehran of backing the Houthi offensive, a charge Iran has publicly denied, saying it supports Yemeni unity and a UN-backed political process. A 2022 UN-brokered truce had largely paused years of civil conflict in Yemen, but clashes resumed in July and have widened steadily since, creating what analysts describe as a second active front tied to the wider regional confrontation.

The consequences are already being felt beyond Yemen's borders. Saudi Arabia has reported Houthi missile and drone attacks on its own territory, including strikes near Khamis Mushait, Abha and Jazan that Riyadh says wounded dozens of people this week. Shipping analysts and energy traders are watching the situation closely, given that any disruption to traffic through Bab el-Mandeb would compound pressure already building on global oil markets from the parallel standoff in the Gulf.

For now, neither the Houthis nor the Saudi-backed government have signalled willingness to pull back from the fight for Yemen's western coastline. With government forces regrouping south of Mocha and Houthi units pushing towards Dhubab and the nearby Hanish Islands, control of the strait's approaches looks set to remain contested in the days ahead.
