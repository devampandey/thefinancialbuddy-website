Parliament completed the passage of the Bankers' Book Evidence Bill on Monday evening after the Rajya Sabha cleared the legislation by voice vote, days after the Lok Sabha had already approved it. The bill now awaits presidential assent before it formally replaces the 135-year-old Bankers' Book Evidence Act of 1891, a colonial-era law that had governed how financial records could be produced as evidence in Indian courts.

## Why A 19th-Century Law Needed Replacing

The 1891 Act was written for a paper-based banking system, allowing certified copies of physical ledgers to stand in for original bank records during legal proceedings. That framework never anticipated a world of core banking systems, cloud storage and digital signatures. Finance Minister Nirmala Sitharaman, replying to the debate in the Upper House, said the update was overdue given how completely record-keeping in the banking sector has moved online.

According to her remarks, the new law is built to be technology-neutral. It formally recognises electronic and digital banking records as admissible evidence, standardises how such records are certified, and allows authentication through manual, digital or electronic signatures. Bank records stored onsite, offsite, on virtual cloud infrastructure, or even in backup and disaster-recovery systems will now carry the same evidentiary weight as a paper ledger once did.

## Relief For Bank Officers, With A Caveat

One of the more practical grievances the bill addresses is the practice of courts summoning bank executives to personally produce physical records, even in disputes where the bank itself was not a party to the case. Sitharaman argued there was no justification for pulling bank officers into litigation that had nothing to do with their institution, and the new law builds in statutory protection against such summons by requiring courts to record the existence of a specific exemption clause.

That said, the bill is not a blanket shield. A separate provision preserves the court's ability to compel a bank's appearance in select circumstances even when it is not formally a party to a dispute, giving judges some discretion where digital records alone may not settle a matter.

## What Else Is In The Fine Print

Officials said the legislation runs to roughly sixteen clauses covering the mechanics of digital evidence admission, including provisions that let the central government extend the same evidentiary framework to other regulated financial entities beyond traditional banks through a simple notification, rather than requiring fresh legislation each time. That flexibility is designed to future-proof the law as digital lending platforms, payment banks and other financial intermediaries expand their footprint.

## Opposition Walks Out Before Full Debate

While the bill passed comfortably by voice vote, Opposition members staged a walkout in the Rajya Sabha before a detailed discussion could take place, part of a broader pattern of disruption that has marked the final stretch of the Monsoon Session. Several opposition parties have been pressing the government on unrelated matters, including a demand for a statement from the Home Minister on a police action against student protesters last month, and those tensions have repeatedly spilled into the passage of otherwise uncontroversial bills.

With the bill now cleared by both Houses, attention turns to how quickly banks, courts and financial regulators adapt their processes to the new digital-evidence standard, and whether the government uses its notification powers to widen the law's reach across the financial sector in the months ahead.
