The Union Cabinet on Wednesday, November 4, approved Rs 14,500 crore of viability gap funding (VGF) to build 92 gigawatt-hours (GWh) of grid-scale energy storage between FY27 and FY31. In the same meeting it cleared a separate Rs 2,814 crore capital subsidy for four critical mineral processing plants, two for lithium and two for nickel. Information and Broadcasting Minister Ashwini Vaishnaw announced both decisions. Read together, they target the biggest weakness in India's clean-energy build-out: the country can now generate large amounts of solar power at midday, but it cannot yet store enough of it for the evening peak, and it imports almost all the refined materials that batteries need.

## What the storage scheme covers

The 92 GWh is split across three technologies. Pumped storage hydro, where water is pumped uphill when power is cheap and released through turbines when demand rises, takes the largest share at 60 GWh. Battery energy storage systems (BESS) account for 30 GWh, and the remaining 2 GWh is set aside for emerging technologies.

Battery projects will have to meet a minimum domestic content requirement of 40%. Public and private electricity distribution companies can claim VGF for battery and pumped storage projects, while projects using alternative storage technologies can be run by public or private entities. The government says the scheme will raise India's grid-scale storage capacity by about 170% and help meet an estimated requirement of 411 GWh by 2031-32. Vaishnaw said the scheme will be rolled out with state governments so that capacity is spread across the country rather than concentrated in a few states.

## Why storage has become urgent

India has added solar capacity much faster than it has added ways to use that power after sunset. The result is a familiar pattern for grid operators: cheap surplus power in the afternoon, when solar output peaks, followed by a steep climb in demand after dark that is still met largely by coal and gas. Without storage, more solar mostly means more afternoon curtailment and very little relief for the evening peak.

The new scheme builds on earlier, smaller efforts. The first VGF scheme for batteries, approved in 2023, aimed for about 4 GWh with Rs 3,760 crore of budget support, and a further battery tranche followed in 2025. The Rs 14,500 crore approved now is several times larger than the 2023 outlay and, by bringing pumped hydro into the same framework, covers the longer-duration storage that batteries alone cannot economically provide.

## The critical minerals piece

The Rs 2,814 crore Capex Support Scheme to Promote Critical Mineral Processing will cover 30% of eligible investment in four plants. Support is capped at Rs 500 crore for each lithium project and Rs 900 crore for each nickel project, and the scheme also runs from FY27 to FY31.

The scheme addresses the middle step of the supply chain. India has spent the last few years trying to secure mining rights for lithium and other minerals at home and abroad, and it already offers incentives for battery cell manufacturing. What sits in between, turning ore or concentrate into battery-grade chemicals, is dominated globally by Chinese refiners. Unless that step is built at home, Indian cell makers will still depend on imported processed material even if the raw ore comes from Indian or partner-country mines.

## What this means

For power distribution companies, the storage subsidy lowers the cost of a tool they increasingly need. A simple average works out to roughly Rs 158 crore of support per GWh, or about Rs 15.8 lakh per MWh. The real split will differ by technology, since pumped hydro and batteries have very different cost profiles. The size of the outlay signals that the Centre wants storage tenders to clear at tariffs states can afford, not just to sit in pipelines.

The heavy tilt towards pumped hydro makes sense on cost and duration. Pumped storage can discharge for six to eight hours or more and lasts for decades, which suits India's long evening peak. The trade-off is time. These projects need suitable sites, environmental clearances and several years of civil construction, and India's hydro projects have a long record of delays. If the 60 GWh slips, the 2031-32 target slips with it. Battery projects, by contrast, can typically be built in 12 to 18 months, which makes the 30 GWh battery share the part most likely to show results within the scheme's five-year window.

The 40% domestic content rule supports local cell and pack makers. It may also raise project costs in the early years, while Indian cell capacity is still ramping up. How strictly that rule is applied, and whether imported cells with Indian assembly count towards it, will decide how much storage the subsidy actually buys.

On minerals, Rs 2,814 crore for four plants is a modest sum, and the per-project caps show that the government expects private or state-owned companies to carry most of the risk. The open questions are who bids, where feedstock comes from, and whether Indian refineries can compete on cost with established Chinese processors. A plant that relies on imported concentrate would still reduce strategic risk, but by less than the headline suggests.

For investors, the decisions improve visibility for companies in pumped storage construction, battery integration and power equipment, as well as hydro-focused state utilities. The actual benefit will depend on how quickly the ministries publish operational guidelines and how fast states run tenders. Those details, not Wednesday's approval, will decide whether this becomes a real build-out or another scheme that underspends. The first pumped storage and battery tenders under the new framework will be the clearest early signal.
