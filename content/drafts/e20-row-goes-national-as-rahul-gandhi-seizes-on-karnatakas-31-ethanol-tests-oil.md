A state-level quarrel over fuel quality became a national political fight on Saturday, November 7. Congress leader Rahul Gandhi used Karnataka government tests to attack the Centre's E20 petrol programme, saying "E20 turned into 420", a reference to the old penal code section for cheating. The tests found that 11 of 14 petrol samples from seven districts had more than the permitted 20% ethanol, with an Indian Oil outlet at 31.2% and a Hindustan Petroleum outlet at 30.5%. Hours earlier, Indian Oil, Bharat Petroleum and Hindustan Petroleum had rejected the findings, saying the samples were not collected or tested the way the law requires. The answer matters to every petrol vehicle owner and to investors in the three listed oil marketing companies (OMCs), and it could shape how far India pushes ethanol beyond E20.

## What Karnataka found

Karnataka's Food, Civil Supplies and Legal Metrology Minister Rizwan Arshad announced the results on Friday. Samples came from Bengaluru, Bengaluru Rural, Kolar, Chikkaballapura, Ramanagara, Mangaluru and Tumakuru. The state says it first sent samples to the oil companies' own labs, which did not report a clear ethanol figure. It then sent them to the Shriram Research Institute in Bengaluru, a private lab accredited by NABL. Readings above the cap covered public and private brands alike: up to 31.2% at IOC, 30.5% and 27% at HPCL, 26.5% at BPCL, 26.1% at Nayara and 25.5% at Shell.

The state then put a price on it. If over-blending cuts mileage by 10%, it says, Indian motorists lose about Rs 62,000 crore a year, plus about Rs 39,000 crore in extra repairs. Congress spokesperson Pawan Khera argued that buyers pay for 80% petrol but get less than 70%.

## The oil companies' objection

The three public sector OMCs say their E20 meets IS 17021, the Bureau of Indian Standards specification, which allows 19% to 21% ethanol. Their objection is mainly about procedure. The 2005 Motor Spirit and High Speed Diesel Control Order, read with the 2024 Marketing Discipline Guidelines, requires six one-litre samples per check. Two go to the dealer, two are kept by the oil company or the department, and two go to a notified laboratory. Labels must be signed jointly.

The OMCs say their staff were not present during Karnataka's October sampling. They also say they never received the retained samples, outlet-wise details or full lab reports. In their view, a certificate from a third-party lab that is not notified under the Control Order cannot be checked independently, and dealers lose their legal right to a retest. Indian Oil says 893 samples tested across 27 states and Union Territories, including 56 from Karnataka, and all fell within the 19-21% band. The companies have asked for joint testing with the state.

## Background: why E20 is sensitive

Ethanol blending, a flagship policy since the 2018 National Policy on Biofuels, has saved more than Rs 1.97 lakh crore in foreign exchange since 2014-15, according to Petroleum Ministry figures cited by the OMCs. Owners of older vehicles have long worried about mileage loss and wear on parts. In August, automakers' body SIAM withdrew a letter that had raised fuel contamination concerns ([our earlier report](/business/auto-industry-body-siam-withdraws-letter-flagging-e20-fuel-contamination-concerns)). Karnataka's tests reopen that argument with specific numbers attached.

## What this means

The two sides are making different kinds of claims. Karnataka has specific readings from named outlets. The OMCs have a procedural objection and a large national sample. Neither settles the question alone, and both can be partly right. A few pumps can have badly blended fuel even when the national average is on target.

Two checks help. The first is national volume. In the 2025-26 ethanol supply year, which ended on October 31, the three OMCs blended 1,134 crore litres of ethanol against roughly 5,673 crore litres of petrol sold. That works out to 19.99%, almost exactly the target. Widespread blending at 30% would need far more ethanol than the companies bought or blended. So the data does not support the idea of deliberate, nationwide over-blending. It cannot rule out errors at particular depots, contamination, or problems with how samples were drawn at particular pumps.

The second check is chemistry. A litre of ethanol holds roughly two-thirds of the energy in a litre of petrol. By our estimate, moving from E20 to E30 cuts the energy in each litre by about 3.5-4%, not 10%. Khera is right that a 70/30 mix has about 12.5% less petrol than an 80/20 mix. But the ethanol still burns, so the mileage loss is much smaller than that figure suggests. Karnataka's Rs 62,000 crore estimate rests on a 10% mileage drop that the fuel chemistry does not support, even if the 31% readings turn out to be accurate. Engine wear in vehicles not built for high blends is a separate, more serious and harder-to-measure concern.

For investors, the immediate financial risk to IOC, BPCL and HPCL looks small. Any lapse found so far is local, and the national data supports the companies. The bigger risk is to their reputation and to policy. The Centre is working towards flex-fuel vehicles and blends above E20. Public distrust of E20 now would make that harder to sell. It could also strengthen calls for pumps to label ethanol content, a point Gandhi raised.

What is still unclear: why Karnataka sent only 14 of its 63 samples to the private lab, how those 14 were chosen, what test method the lab used, and why the OMCs' labs did not report ethanol figures for the earlier samples. The quickest way to settle this is a joint retest at a notified laboratory, with signed samples and published results. A side that refuses weakens its own case. Watch whether the Petroleum Ministry orders an audit, whether other states run their own tests, and whether Karnataka hands over the documents the OMCs have asked for.
