Bank branches across India go quiet for three days starting Monday, September 28, and most coverage of it is framing this as a fight over a long weekend. Nine unions under the United Forum of Bank Unions, representing close to 90% of the workforce across public sector banks, regional rural banks, and some older private banks, are striking for a five-day work week. That demand is real and it is old — banks have run on alternate Saturdays off since 2015, and full weekends were actually agreed in principle in the March 2024 wage settlement. It just never got implemented. I don't think that's the interesting part of this story, though. The interesting part is the second demand riding alongside it, which barely makes the headlines: a fight over the Performance-Linked Incentive scheme, and what it says about how bank employees will get paid from here on.

## Why September 30, specifically

This is the second strike in three weeks — there was already a one-day walkout on September 11 that changed nothing — and this time the unions picked their date with intent. September 30 is the half-year book closing for Indian banks: the day reconciliation, provisioning, and treasury positions get settled. Layer the strike onto the preceding weekend and you get branch-level disruption across five straight days, not three. The Finance Ministry's response tells you how seriously it's taking that math: it directed public sector banks and RRBs to open on Sunday, September 27, an otherwise routine holiday, specifically to claw back one of those five days. UFBU is now disputing that move too, along with a separate fight over which categories of staff the Indian Banks' Association can exempt from striking as "sensitive installations." Both sides are negotiating with the calendar as much as with each other.

## The demand nobody's really covering

Here's what I think gets lost. The five-day week is a *conditions* fight — how many days you work. The PLI dispute is a *structure* fight — how you get paid, and whether that stays collective or becomes individual. The revised incentive scheme, introduced in November 2024, applies only to officers at Scale IV and above. Everyone below that continues under the old collectively bargained pay structure; everyone above it now has a slice of compensation tied to individual or branch performance metrics set by management, outside the union agreement. Unions call this discriminatory, and structurally, I think they're right to treat it as the bigger threat: it's the first real crack in the idea that a public sector bank employee's pay is something negotiated as a bloc, not assigned from above. The five-day week, if it ever lands, changes a calendar. The PLI precedent, if it holds, changes what collective bargaining in Indian banking is even for.

## What actually breaks for the rest of us

If you bank with SBI, PNB, Bank of Baroda, or most regional rural banks, the disruption is real but narrow: cash deposits and withdrawals at the counter, cheque clearance, fresh FD or loan paperwork, and locker access are what's affected. UPI, IMPS, net banking, mobile apps, and ATMs are expected to keep running — these don't depend on a branch employee showing up. The exception is ATM cash replenishment, which does depend on branch staff, so a five-day stretch of disruption is the scenario where you'd actually notice a cash-out at a machine near you. If you bank with HDFC, ICICI, Axis, or most other private banks, none of this touches you; they sit outside UFBU entirely. The practical advice going around — clear pending cheques and cash needs before September 26 — is sound, and I'd follow it if you have anything time-sensitive due that week.

## Where I think this goes

The IBA's public position has been blunt: banks "cannot afford to provide lesser number of working days." That's not the language of a side preparing to concede, and the government has already said a five-day week won't happen this financial year. I don't expect September 28-30 to produce a breakthrough. What I'd actually watch is October 26 — UFBU has already flagged an indefinite nationwide strike from that date if nothing moves — because an indefinite strike, unlike a three-day one, is the point where an employer has to negotiate rather than just wait it out. Until then, this week's strike is less a resolution than a pressure test: both sides finding out how much a calendar-timed disruption actually costs the other. My money is on the PLI fight, not the Saturday fight, being the one that still matters a year from now.
