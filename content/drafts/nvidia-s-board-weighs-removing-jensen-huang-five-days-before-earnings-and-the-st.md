Nvidia's board of directors met late on Wednesday, November 11, to decide whether to remove Jensen Huang, the co-founder who has run the company since 1993. The Wall Street Journal first reported that the board was preparing to oust him, and Reuters confirmed the meeting through a person familiar with the matter. Nvidia shares, which had closed 2.92% higher at $173.73 in regular trading, fell 8.61% to $158.78 after hours, at one point losing as much as 10%. If that decline holds when US markets open, the world's most valuable chipmaker will have shed roughly $600 billion of market value since Sunday. As of Thursday morning India time, no outcome had been announced. The question matters well beyond one company: Nvidia's chips sit at the centre of the global AI build-out, including India's.

## How a governance probe became a succession crisis

The turn has been fast. On Sunday, November 8, Nvidia's board said a committee of independent directors, working with outside counsel, would investigate whether Huang had been the target of a foreign intelligence effort. The trigger was his relationship with a businesswoman, Alice Huang (no relation), whom US intelligence officials suspect of spying for China and of helping move Nvidia chips to Chinese companies. At that point the board said it had no reason to believe company secrets or US national security had been compromised, and it publicly backed Huang and his team. In a memo to staff the same day, Huang admitted being unfaithful to his wife and said he took full responsibility, without giving details.

On Wednesday, the New York Times reported a second relationship, this one with Kimberly Powell, Nvidia's vice-president of healthcare, who reports directly to him. Within hours, the board was reported to be discussing his removal. Under Nvidia's bylaws, a majority of directors can remove the CEO with or without cause. Ten of the eleven directors are classed as independent; Huang is the eleventh.

Political pressure has also built up. Senator Kevin Cramer has called for Huang to resign, and Huang could face a congressional subpoena if he does not agree to a closed-door briefing. Lawmakers are also using the episode to push for tighter limits on chip exports to China.

## No obvious heir

Huang, 63, holds about 3.6% of Nvidia and has dozens of direct reports, so the company is unusually built around one person. Bloomberg reported that the board has hired an outside firm to identify possible replacements and is considering an interim chief executive. Investors quoted by Reuters named chief financial officer Colette Kress, who has held that role for more than a decade, as the natural interim choice. Prediction markets favour Ian Buck, who runs the data-centre business, as a longer-term successor.

The timing is awkward: Nvidia reports quarterly results on November 17, so whoever is in charge will have to face investors within days.

## How markets reacted

The selloff spread quickly but unevenly. Nasdaq 100 futures fell about 1% and S&P 500 futures slipped 0.4% in Asian hours. CoreWeave, an AI cloud company that depends heavily on Nvidia hardware, fell 6% after hours, while rivals AMD and Broadcom rose 4% and 3% respectively. In China, chip stocks rose on bets that local suppliers will benefit: SMIC gained 2.4% in Shanghai, Hua Hong rose 2.7% and equipment maker NAURA added 3.1%. Indian benchmarks barely moved, with the Nifty 50 up about 0.1% and the Sensex up 0.2% in early trade.

## What this means

For most Indian investors the direct hit is small but real. Anyone holding US tech through international mutual fund schemes, Nasdaq-linked ETFs or direct US accounts has meaningful Nvidia exposure, because the stock is one of the largest weights in US large-cap and technology indices. An 8% fall in one stock does not wreck a diversified fund. But it is a reminder that "global diversification" through US tech is, in practice, a heavy bet on a handful of companies.

The bigger issue is what a leaderless Nvidia means for the AI spending cycle. Demand for its chips is unlikely to fall in the next few quarters; buyers have few alternatives at scale. The risk is to the wider network of companies Nvidia has financed or partnered with, many of them customers building data centres. India is now part of that network. Projects such as Larsen & Toubro's [10,000-GPU Nvidia facility in Chennai](/technology/lt-to-build-10000-gpu-nvidia-ai-factory-in-chennai-for-together-ai) depend on stable supply and long-term product plans. Those plans have, for three decades, been set by Huang personally.

Two clear winners stand out if the turmoil drags on. One is AMD, along with custom-chip makers like Broadcom, which gain leverage with hyperscalers looking to reduce their reliance on Nvidia. The other is China's domestic chip industry, which benefits whether or not Washington tightens export rules, because Chinese buyers have another reason to switch.

There is also a macro backdrop that matters for India. Fed funds futures are pricing an 87% chance that the US Federal Reserve raises rates by 25 basis points on December 9, and the dollar is near an 18-month high, with the rupee hovering around 93.7. A sharp derisking in US tech on top of tighter US policy would test the foreign inflows that have recently lifted Indian equities.

Several things are still unclear. Whether the board removes Huang or keeps him with reduced powers and stronger oversight is unknown. Nothing so far shows that Nvidia technology was compromised. Our view: replacing a founder-CEO at a $4 trillion company in the middle of an AI investment cycle carries real execution risk. The market's sharp reaction suggests investors already see his departure as the likely outcome rather than a tail risk.

What to watch next: any formal board statement or SEC filing, the name of an interim chief if one is appointed, the November 17 earnings call and its guidance, and whether Washington moves on tighter chip export rules.
