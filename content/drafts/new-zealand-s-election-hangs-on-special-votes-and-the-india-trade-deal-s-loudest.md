New Zealand voted on Saturday, November 7, and nobody won outright. With about 98% of the election-night count done, Prime Minister Christopher Luxon's National Party and its two coalition partners, ACT and New Zealand First, were projected to hold 67 seats in a 131-seat Parliament. That is one seat more than the 66 needed to govern. Labour and National each took just under 30% of the party vote. Between 500,000 and 700,000 special votes are still uncounted, and these have historically leaned left, so the Electoral Commission's final result on November 27 could still change the outcome. For India, the result matters because the India-New Zealand free trade agreement came into force only on October 20. One of the parties now holding the balance, NZ First, is the deal's best-known opponent.

## How the numbers stand

Preliminary figures put National on 42 seats, ACT on 13 and NZ First on 12, a combined 67. On the other side, Labour has 36, the Greens a record 16 and Te Pāti Māori 5, a total of 57. The new centrist Opportunity party cleared the 5% threshold needed to enter Parliament and is projected to win 7 seats. That would make it the first new party in Parliament in more than a decade.

Parliament is larger than its nominal 120 seats because of overhang. When a party wins more electorate seats than its share of the party vote would give it, the extra seats are added on top. Te Pāti Māori asked supporters to give it their electorate vote and give their party vote to its left-leaning allies, precisely to create this effect.

Luxon told supporters in Auckland the result was "too close to call", while saying National was again the largest party. Labour leader Chris Hipkins did not concede. He said New Zealand had spoken but that it would take time to work out what it had said. During the campaign, Luxon ruled out governing with Opportunity because of its tax policy. Its leader, Qiulae Wong, has said she would talk to National first because it won the most seats.

## Why the special votes matter

Special votes are cast by people living overseas, people travelling, and people voting outside their home electorate. A shift of a fraction of a percentage point can move a list seat from one bloc to the other. In 2017, the final count cost National two seats, one each to Labour and the Greens. The coalition's current margin is one seat, so a similar swing would reduce it to a minority. The left would still fall short on its own numbers, and that would put Opportunity's 7 seats at the centre of negotiations.

Until the result is declared, Luxon stays on as caretaker prime minister. Coalition talks cannot properly begin until the seat totals are final.

## The India angle: a deal already in force

India and New Zealand signed their free trade agreement in New Delhi on April 27, 2026, through Commerce Minister Piyush Goyal and New Zealand Trade Minister Todd McClay. New Zealand's Parliament passed the enabling law in September by 93 votes to 29, with both National and Labour in favour, as we [reported at the time](/world/new-zealand-parliament-passes-fta-legislation-with-india). The agreement took effect on October 20.

Under the deal, every Indian export to New Zealand enters duty-free. About 57% of New Zealand's exports to India became tariff-free immediately, and cuts eventually extend to around 95%. Major dairy products such as milk, cheese and butter were left out, at India's insistence. On people movement, the agreement creates new temporary employment visa routes with a cap of 5,000 skilled Indian professionals at any one time, expands post-study work rights for some Indian graduates, and commits to a working holiday scheme within two years.

NZ First leader Winston Peters, who is also foreign minister, attacked the deal when it was concluded in December 2025. He called it "neither free nor fair", objected to the dairy exclusion and the visa concessions, and said his party would vote against the enabling law. The deal went through only because Labour voted with National.

## What this means

The trade deal itself is not in danger. Both major parties voted for it, it is already in force, and neither Luxon nor Hipkins campaigned on reopening it. Indian exporters of textiles, engineering goods, pharmaceuticals and processed food can plan on the tariff terms holding, whoever governs.

The risk is in implementation, not the treaty. If Luxon returns with a one-seat majority, NZ First's 12 seats become more powerful than before. NZ First's main objection was immigration, and the visa provisions are the part of the agreement that depends most on government action: setting up the new visa categories, designing the working holiday scheme, and deciding how quickly applications are processed. A smaller partner that cannot block a treaty can still ask for slower, tighter rules in exchange for its votes on other matters. Indian students and IT and healthcare professionals looking at New Zealand should watch for this.

A Labour-led government, if the special votes deliver one, would probably be more relaxed on the visa side. But it would depend on the Greens and Te Pāti Māori, and possibly Opportunity, whose priorities are mostly domestic: housing, the cost of living and tax. India's trade relationship is unlikely to be a negotiating point for any of them.

Several questions remain open. Will the special votes move a seat? Will Luxon drop his refusal to work with Opportunity if he needs to? How hard will NZ First press on immigration in a second coalition agreement? Our view is that Indian businesses should not expect disruption to tariffs, but anyone relying on the new visa routes should expect them to arrive slowly and cautiously if the current coalition holds on. The date to watch is November 27, followed by the coalition agreements, which in New Zealand are published in full.
