Germany's version of the youth protest wave that has rattled France, Italy and other European countries got under way on Saturday afternoon, with rallies organised under the banner "Gen Z regelt" (roughly, "Gen Z has got this") starting in more than 30 cities. The early picture, however, was of a movement whose online reach is running well ahead of its street presence.

## Sign-ups versus turnout

By Saturday, around 180,000 people had clicked "I'm in" on the initiative's website, spread across 34 locations. Cologne led with more than 16,000 registrations, followed by Frankfurt with about 14,000, Berlin with over 12,000 and Munich with more than 9,000.

The crowds that actually gathered at the 2 pm start were much thinner. In Frankfurt, where police had planned for 2,000 people, officers counted roughly 1,000 at the opening rally on the Weseler Werft riverbank before organisers from two groups merged into a single march through the city centre. In Cologne, only a few dozen people had assembled an hour before the start, though local reporters described the Rhine embankment filling to several thousand once speeches began. Berlin police put early attendance at the Brandenburg Gate at around 2,700, against 10,000 announced by organisers. Leipzig drew about 1,500, while in Bielefeld organisers themselves expected around 3,000 of the 6,500 who had registered.

## What the protesters want

The demands are broad: more money for crumbling schools and universities, measures against rising rents and living costs, stronger climate action, and opposition to a possible return of compulsory military service. Placards in Berlin and Hamburg repeatedly linked defence spending to cuts elsewhere, with one mother telling reporters she did not want her 13-year-old son worrying about being sent to war. Chants against Chancellor Friedrich Merz were heard in Leipzig and Cologne.

Organisers have repeatedly said they reject violence and extremist groups, and have distanced themselves from a separate, later rally in Berlin's Rosenthaler Platz called by other left-wing groups.

## Shops braced for trouble

The run-up was dominated by fears of a repeat of the looting and arson seen at youth protests in France earlier this month. Luxury stores in Berlin, Düsseldorf, Hamburg and Frankfurt boarded up their windows. Jeweller Wempe shut all its German branches for the day, and perfume chain Douglas closed a dozen stores. In Stuttgart, authorities rerouted the march away from the main shopping streets after traders wrote to the city, and the first flares were lit there shortly after the start. Hesse's interior ministry said around 80 violence-prone left-wing extremists could travel to Frankfurt, and big-city police forces each deployed more than 1,000 officers.

In the first hours, rallies were largely peaceful, and police in Munich noted that shopkeepers had boarded up windows on their own initiative rather than on police advice.

## Why it matters beyond Germany

The protests come as governments across Europe juggle higher defence budgets, sticky inflation and tight public finances. France has deployed 70,000 police for another weekend of demonstrations, and Italy saw more than 100,000 people on the streets on Friday. Germany's turnout gap suggests the movement has not yet found a single unifying cause, but the sheer number of online sign-ups shows how widely the frustration over housing, education and conscription is shared among young voters, a group the Merz government can ill afford to alienate.
