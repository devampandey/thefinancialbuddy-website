The Trump administration has accused more than 40 countries, including India, of participating in what it calls a "shadow transshipment network" that helps Chinese goods enter the United States while dodging tariffs meant to target Beijing directly. The accusation was laid out in a report published Thursday by the White House Office of Trade and Manufacturing Policy, headed by presidential trade counsellor Peter Navarro, and picked up by multiple international outlets early Friday.

## What the report claims

Titled "The Great Transshipment Scam," the report alleges that Chinese exporters have been routing goods through third countries where minor processing, relabelling, repackaging or paperwork changes create the appearance of a different country of origin, while the underlying manufacturing content remains overwhelmingly Chinese. The report estimates the annual value of such illegally transshipped goods at anywhere between $40 billion and over $300 billion, depending on the methodology used, and says the practice has deprived US government coffers of tens of billions of dollars in tariff revenue every year.

Alongside India, the report names the European Union, Mexico, Canada, Japan and South Korea as among China's "biggest enablers." Southeast Asian economies, including Indonesia, Thailand, Malaysia and Cambodia, are also flagged as playing "an important role" in the alleged network. US manufacturing sectors described as hardest hit include electrical equipment, integrated circuits, aluminium products and motor components.

## AI-powered enforcement planned

The White House said it intends to deploy a new artificial intelligence system, described in early reporting as "Detective Border," to cross-reference shipment data, trade routes, product classifications and ownership records in order to flag suspicious discrepancies in declared countries of origin. "The message to the world is simple. The age of untraceable illegal transshipment is over," the trade office said in the report, warning that countries facilitating such routing are being "put on notice."

Neither the Indian government nor China's embassy in Washington had issued a public response to the report by the time of publication. None of the more than 40 countries named have formally reacted to the allegations so far.

## Part of a broader tariff push

The report lands amid an already turbulent year for US trade policy. Trump has pursued a series of protectionist measures since returning to office, including a fresh round of 10-12.5 percent levies announced recently on imports from countries accused of tolerating forced labour in supply chains. A group of 25 Democratic-led US states, including New York, California and Colorado, is separately challenging the administration's tariff powers in court, arguing the measures are a workaround after the Supreme Court struck down the original "Liberation Day" tariffs in February.

Trade experts have suggested the transshipment report should be read in that context. Amitendu Palit, a trade policy professor at the National University of Singapore, was quoted as saying the report is the latest in a series of "innovative" pressure tactics the administration is using to push trading partners toward greater market access for US goods, following earlier Section 301 tariff actions tied to forced-labour concerns.

## Why it matters for India

India has been a significant beneficiary of supply-chain diversification away from China in recent years, with exporters and government officials often citing this shift as a growth opportunity. Being named in a formal US enforcement report tied to alleged tariff evasion could complicate that narrative and invite closer scrutiny of Indian export documentation for goods with Chinese-origin components, even as New Delhi and Washington continue separate negotiations on a broader bilateral trade framework.
