Tata Trusts, the largest shareholder in Tata Sons, on Monday put forward a restructuring plan designed to keep the group's holding company private and out of the stock market. The proposal, led by Tata Trusts chairman Noel Tata, would merge two unlisted Tata businesses into Tata Sons so that it no longer fits the regulatory definition that currently obliges it to list.

## What The Plan Proposes

Under the proposal, Tata Electronics Systems Solutions (TESS), a wholly owned subsidiary of Tata Electronics, and engineering consultancy Tata Consulting Engineers (TCE) would be amalgamated with Tata Sons Pvt Ltd.

The idea is to give Tata Sons substantial operating revenue of its own. According to Tata Trusts, the merged entity would have operating revenue of about ₹1.05 trillion, well above its income from financial assets of ₹40,072 crore. With operating businesses forming the bulk of its income, Tata Sons would no longer meet the principal business test for a non-banking financial company, or the conditions that define a core investment company.

Without those two labels, Tata Trusts argues, the Reserve Bank of India's requirement that large upper-layer NBFCs list on the stock exchanges would no longer apply.

Tata Trusts has written to the Tata Sons board asking it to consider and approve the plan, and to apply to the RBI for the no-objection certificate needed for such an amalgamation. Tata Sons did not comment on the proposal.

## A Return To An Older Model

Tata Trusts has framed the plan as a return to how Tata Sons operated for most of its history rather than a new structure. For nearly 80 of its roughly 100 years, the holding company ran operating businesses of its own, which helped fund newer ventures. Tata Consultancy Services, for instance, was a division of Tata Sons until 2004 before being spun off.

## How The Dispute Got Here

The RBI classified Tata Sons as an upper-layer NBFC in September 2022, which gave it three years to list. The company repaid all its debt in 2024 and applied to surrender its core investment company registration so it could remain private. Earlier this month, the RBI rejected that application and asked the company to comply with the listing norms immediately.

At a board meeting on September 17, Tata Sons backed the listing route suggested by the regulator. Noel Tata, a nominee director on the board, opposed it and argued there was still room to negotiate. The Shapoorji Pallonji group, which holds more than 18% of Tata Sons, supports listing, since it would allow the group to sell part of its stake and reduce its debt.

## Will The RBI Agree?

That is the central question. Having already turned down the company's request to deregister as a core investment company, the regulator may be reluctant to accept a restructuring aimed at achieving a similar result. Governance experts have flagged that getting RBI approval at this stage could be difficult.

The plan also needs support from the Tata Sons board, which only recently voted in favour of listing. That sets up another round in the tussle between the trusts and other stakeholders over the future of India's largest business group.

For investors in listed Tata companies, the outcome matters because a Tata Sons listing has been widely expected to unlock value across group stocks, while a decision to stay private would remove that trigger.
