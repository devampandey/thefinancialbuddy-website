Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelensky travelled to Belgrade on Saturday for his first-ever official visit to Serbia, holding talks with President Aleksandar Vucic at the Palace of Serbia in a meeting notable as much for its symbolism as for its substance.

## Why the Visit Stands Out

Serbia has long maintained closer ties to Moscow than most other European nations, making it an unusual stop on Zelensky's diplomatic circuit more than four years into Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine. The visit comes even as Serbia continues to pursue European Union membership, putting Vucic in the position of balancing his country's historical relationship with Russia against its stated ambition to move closer to Brussels.

Despite hosting Zelensky with what observers described as considerable ceremony, Vucic made clear that Serbia's underlying position on Russia has not shifted. He reiterated that Serbia does not intend to change its stance on sanctions against Moscow, telling reporters that the country's policy "has not changed and this will remain so for the time being."

## What Was on the Agenda

The two leaders discussed expanding economic ties between Ukraine and Serbia, the prospects for both countries' European Union integration, cooperation in the energy sector, and broader security issues tied to the ongoing war. Zelensky was also scheduled to meet separately with Serbian Prime Minister Djuro Macut during his visit, extending the diplomatic engagement beyond his talks with Vucic alone.

For Zelensky, the trip is part of a continuing push to shore up international backing for Ukraine at a moment when Russian strikes on Kyiv and other Ukrainian cities have intensified again. Bringing a Moscow-aligned government like Serbia's into closer dialogue, even without a shift in its sanctions policy, is being read by analysts as an attempt to widen the circle of countries willing to engage directly with Kyiv, rather than only reinforcing ties with existing allies in Western Europe.

## A Balancing Act for Belgrade

For Vucic, hosting Zelensky with visible pomp sends its own signal to both Brussels and Moscow, suggesting Serbia wants to keep its EU accession ambitions alive without appearing to abandon its traditional relationship with Russia. That balancing act has defined much of Serbia's foreign policy since the war began, and Saturday's meeting is unlikely to resolve the tension between those two pulls, even as it marks a symbolically significant first step in direct engagement between the two presidents.

## The Broader Context

The visit takes place against the backdrop of renewed Russian bombardment of Ukrainian cities, with strikes on the Kyiv region in the past day alone causing casualties and damage. Zelensky's outreach to a historically Russia-friendly capital like Belgrade underscores how central diplomatic engagement, alongside battlefield and air-defence developments, has become to Ukraine's strategy as the war continues into its fifth year.
