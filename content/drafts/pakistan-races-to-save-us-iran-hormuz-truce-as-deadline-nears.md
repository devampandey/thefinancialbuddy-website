Pakistan intensified its diplomatic push on Wednesday to keep alive a fragile negotiating window between the United States and Iran, with just days left before a 60-day framework agreed in June expires. The effort comes as Iran's newly appointed security chief made clear that Tehran has no intention of reopening the Strait of Hormuz without major concessions from Washington.

## A four-day countdown

The interim memorandum of understanding brokered by Pakistan and Qatar in mid-June gave Washington and Tehran 60 days to hammer out a fuller agreement. That window closes in a matter of days, and any extension would require both sides to agree. Pakistan's foreign ministry said Islamabad was working "direct and indirect channels" to keep the two sides talking, with Interior Minister Mohsin Naqvi travelling to Tehran and Deputy Prime Minister Ishaq Dar holding calls with counterparts across the Gulf.

Pakistan's defence minister struck an optimistic note, telling reporters that the US and Iran were "close to some sort of arrangement" and that matters were "shaping up in favour of peace." Qatar's foreign ministry echoed the sentiment, saying talks between Oman and Iran over the strait's future management had reached an "advanced stage."

## Iran's conditions remain unchanged

That optimism sits uneasily alongside statements from Mohsen Rezaei, the hardline figure recently named to head Iran's Supreme National Security Council. Rezaei told China's ambassador in Tehran that the Strait of Hormuz would stay closed until Washington ends what Iran calls its blockade of Iranian ports, releases frozen Iranian assets, and agrees to a region-wide ceasefire covering Lebanon and Gaza. Writing separately on social media, he reiterated that no partial steps would be enough.

President Donald Trump, for his part, has insisted the US already has "total control" of the waterway, telling reporters American forces have swept it for mines and secured it. Shipping data tells a more complicated story: tracking firm Kpler reported only eight vessels transited the strait on Tuesday, below the ten-day average and among the lowest daily counts since the war began, with normal pre-war traffic running closer to 130 ships a day.

## Violence complicates the diplomacy

The talks are unfolding against a backdrop of continued attacks on shipping at both ends of the region. A US Navy helicopter fired on a Panama-flagged cargo vessel in the Gulf of Oman this week after it allegedly tried to breach the American blockade of Iranian ports, disabling its steering without reported casualties. Separately, a Houthi strike on a cargo ship in the Bab al-Mandeb strait killed several crew members and rescuers, marking one of the first fatal attacks on shipping there since the broader conflict began.

Analysts say the coming days will reveal whether the tough public rhetoric from both Washington and Tehran reflects genuine red lines or opening positions ahead of a deal. A senior adviser to Iran's Revolutionary Guard told a US broadcaster that Tehran could seek to prolong the conflict as a deterrent against future attacks, underscoring how far apart the two sides remain even as Pakistan works to bring them back to the table.

For India, a prolonged standoff in the strait carries direct consequences: crude oil prices have been climbing for six straight sessions on the uncertainty, a trend that feeds through to domestic fuel costs and the broader inflation outlook.
