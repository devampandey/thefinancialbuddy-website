The United States is prepared to offer Iran sanctions relief and access to frozen assets in return for concrete steps on its nuclear programme, US officials said on Monday, the clearest economic incentive Washington has put on the table in the seven-month war. The signal comes two days after President Donald Trump turned down Tehran's seven-day ceasefire plan ([see our earlier coverage](/world/trump-rejects-irans-seven-day-ceasefire-proposal-eyes-strikes-after-midterms)).

## What Washington is offering

According to officials who spoke to Axios and CNN, the White House would consider lifting some sanctions and releasing Iranian money held abroad, but only after Iran takes verifiable action on its nuclear activities. A US official familiar with the indirect talks described them as positive and constructive, and said Iran has signalled flexibility on nuclear questions.

The same official was blunt about the limits. There will be no agreement unless the nuclear file is addressed, and the two sides still disagree on sequencing: who moves first, and how quickly each side's commitments take effect.

## A new mediation push

The offer lands in the middle of a fresh round of shuttle diplomacy. Qatari and Pakistani mediators were meeting Iranian Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi in New York on Monday to discuss a Qatari proposal negotiated over the past several days. The mediators are expected to brief Trump administration officials later on Monday or on Tuesday.

The gap between the two sides remains wide. Tehran wants any deal to start with the Strait of Hormuz and an end to the US naval blockade of Iranian ports. Its rejected plan had asked Washington to lift the blockade, waive oil sanctions and release roughly 12 billion dollars in frozen assets before Iran reopened the strait. Washington wants nuclear commitments first, which turns the frozen-funds question into a matter of timing rather than principle.

## Leverage and distrust

US officials framed the offer as coming from strength, pointing to rising tanker traffic through Hormuz and continuing economic damage from the blockade and sanctions. Trump said on Sunday that Iran's economy is collapsing and claimed inflation there is running near 300 percent.

The White House also made clear it does not fully trust Tehran. Officials accused Iran of breaking an earlier memorandum of understanding by firing on commercial ships in the strait in July, and said the US needs firmer assurances this time that Iran is serious rather than simply looking for a way out of a tight spot. Trump has also declined to rule out further military strikes before the US midterm elections in November.

## Why it matters for India

For India, which imports most of its crude, the stalemate has a direct price. Indian equities slid to a six-month low on Monday as Brent climbed on the deadlock, extending a seven-session losing streak. A sequenced deal that eases the blockade and reopens Hormuz would likely cool oil prices quickly, easing pressure on the rupee, fuel costs and inflation. Until the question of who moves first is resolved, though, Monday's offer is a signal of intent rather than a breakthrough.
