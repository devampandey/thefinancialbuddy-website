Christine Lagarde will leave the presidency of the European Central Bank on February 7, 2027, roughly nine months before her eight-year term was due to end in October 2027. The ECB said on Thursday, October 29, that she had informed its Governing Council and European Council President António Costa of the decision.

A few hours later, the bank she is leaving kept its deposit rate unchanged at 2.5% and warned that the inflation picture was getting worse, not better.

## An exit long rumoured, timed with France in mind

Lagarde gave no public reason for leaving early. Speculation about her departure had been building for months, with the World Economic Forum widely seen as her likely next stop. Reuters reported earlier this month that the WEF could name her its chair as soon as November.

The timing matters politically. Leaving in February gives the euro area's governments room to agree on a successor well before France holds its presidential election in spring 2027, a contest in which polls show far-right leader Marine Le Pen as the favourite. A new ECB chief chosen after that vote could face a very different set of negotiations among member states.

The early frontrunners are two former national central bank governors: Klaas Knot of the Netherlands and Pablo Hernández de Cos of Spain. The choice will be made by the 21 countries that use the euro. It will not be the only change at the top. Chief economist Philip Lane and executive board member Isabel Schnabel are also due to leave next year, so the bank faces an unusually broad turnover of senior leadership within a few months.

## The rate decision: on hold, leaning hawkish

The hold itself was expected. The ECB has already raised rates twice this year, trying to stop an energy price shock linked to the Iran war from spreading into wages and wider prices. Inflation is running at around twice its 2% target.

The bank's statement leaned firmly toward further tightening. It said energy prices had continued to rise, creating upside risks to inflation and downside risks to growth. It described underlying inflation as broadly stable and said wages had not yet responded materially, but warned that the larger and longer energy shock was likely to push inflation up and weigh on the economy. Markets read that as keeping a December hike firmly in play. Most economists polled by Reuters expect one or two more increases before the cycle ends.

## The French bond problem

The harder issue for whoever runs the ECB next is government borrowing costs. A global climb in bond yields has hit debt-heavy France hardest. Paris now pays about 155 basis points more than Germany to borrow for ten years, the widest gap in decades. The ECB acknowledged that financing conditions had tightened since its last meeting and said this would likely weigh on both growth and inflation.

That leaves the bank on a narrow path. Raising rates further could deepen stress in French bonds, while holding back risks letting inflation settle at a higher level. Its first tool, if needed, would likely be flexible reinvestment of maturing bonds from past purchase programmes. Beyond that sits the Transmission Protection Instrument, which has never been used.

## Why it matters beyond Europe

With the US Federal Reserve also holding rates this week and keeping a hike on the table ([see our Fed coverage](/world/fed-holds-at-3-75-4-in-its-last-decision-before-the-midterms-leaving-a-december)), two of the world's largest central banks are now signalling that the next move is more likely up than down. For emerging markets such as India, that combination tends to keep global yields elevated and makes foreign portfolio money harder to attract. A leadership change at the ECB during a tightening cycle adds one more source of uncertainty for global bond markets in the months ahead.
