Karnataka observed a statewide bandh on Thursday, with Kannada organisations and farmer groups protesting a directive from the Cauvery Water Management Authority (CWMA) ordering the state to release 12,000 cusecs of water daily to Tamil Nadu for a 15-day period starting August 12.

The shutdown, called for roughly 12 hours from 6 am to 6 pm, is the latest flashpoint in the decades-old dispute between Karnataka and Tamil Nadu over sharing the Cauvery river's waters. Protest organisers argue the CWMA's order comes at a time when Karnataka's own reservoirs are under strain and accuse the state government of failing to adequately contest the release mandate before the authority.

Despite the call for a bandh, essential services largely continued to function normally. Schools, banks, hospitals, pharmacies and public transport operated without major disruption across the state, according to local reporting, and private vehicles including app-based cab services also ran largely as usual, with no widespread reports of passengers being stranded. The Karnataka Association of Management of Schools had initially urged institutions to shut for the day but later withdrew that call, instead advising schools to use the day to educate students about the Cauvery dispute rather than close entirely.

The political temperature around the bandh rose through the day. Kannada activist Vatal Nagraj had insisted the shutdown would proceed as planned despite pushback from some quarters. Later, Karnataka Chief Minister D.K. Shivakumar said Kannada activist groups had met with him and agreed to call off the bandh, though normal life and traffic patterns in Bengaluru appeared largely unaffected regardless, suggesting the protest's practical impact on the ground was limited even as it dominated headlines and political discourse.

Security was stepped up in parts of the state as a precaution, with reports of some protesters being detained during the day. The episode underscores how the Cauvery dispute continues to carry outsized political weight in Karnataka, where water-sharing has periodically triggered unrest since the early 1990s and remains a sensitive issue capable of uniting otherwise disparate political and civil society groups.

For businesses operating in Karnataka, particularly in Bengaluru's technology and services corridors, bandh days typically raise questions about productivity and logistics even when disruption proves modest, as appears to have been the case on Thursday. Companies with operations tied to road transport, such as logistics and quick-commerce firms, tend to be watched most closely on such days, though no major reports emerged of supply chain disruption linked to Thursday's protest.

The broader Cauvery dispute, which has periodically gone before the Supreme Court and specialised tribunals over the years, shows no sign of a durable resolution. With the current CWMA order running through the end of August, further friction between the two states remains possible in the near term.
