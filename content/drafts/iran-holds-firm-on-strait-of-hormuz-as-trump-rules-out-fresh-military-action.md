The standoff over the Strait of Hormuz showed no signs of easing over the weekend, with Iran reiterating tough conditions for reopening the vital oil shipping route even as US President Donald Trump ruled out any fresh military offensive against Tehran, describing Washington's current posture as "low-key."

## Iran's Demands Echo the June Memorandum

Iran's position was laid out on Saturday by Mohammad Bagher Zolghadr, secretary of the country's Supreme National Security Council, who called on the United States to lift its naval blockade and sanctions on Iranian oil and petrochemical exports, withdraw US forces stationed near Iran, pay war reparations, release frozen Iranian assets, and end what Tehran describes as attacks on its regional allies, including Lebanon. Analysts note that, apart from the reparations demand, these conditions largely mirror commitments Trump himself agreed to in a memorandum of understanding signed on June 17.

Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi said Sunday that talks with Oman over technical arrangements for shipping through the strait were "progressing well" and in their "final stages," with experts working through detailed maps of the route. However, he cautioned that a technical agreement with Muscat "does not mean reopening the strait," which he said remains contingent on Washington fulfilling conditions conveyed through intermediaries. Separately, Araghchi indicated Iran was not yet in direct talks with the United States, relying instead on messages passed through mediators, even as he said "efforts are underway to create grounds for negotiations."

Analysts tracking the standoff say Iran views control of the strait as its strongest point of leverage. "Iranian officials are concerned that if the strait opens, Trump would walk away from this conflict without feeling the need to resolve it," said Esfandyar Batmanghelidj of the Bourse & Bazaar Foundation, a think tank focused on economic diplomacy. Ali Vaez of the International Crisis Group described the strait as a "sword of Damocles" hanging over Trump, while Sanam Vakil of Chatham House pointed to internal divisions within Iran's leadership over whether to prioritise diplomacy or continue withholding cooperation as a bargaining chip.

## Trump Downplays Military Option, Cites Iran's Economy

Speaking to reporters on Sunday, Trump said the United States was taking a "low-key" approach toward Iran and was only "semi-negotiating," while closely watching what he described as Iran's worsening economic situation. "We are just watching Iran with its huge inflation and the fact they have no money," Trump said, adding that the country was "in very bad shape" and struggling even to pay its troops, a strain he attributed partly to the ongoing US naval blockade.

Trump also noted that oil prices, hovering a little above $75 a barrel by his account, had softened the impact of the standoff on American consumers, and characterised the broader negotiation as unpredictable but ultimately resolvable. "It will work out. It always works out. It's like a chess game," he said.

Even as Trump played down the prospect of renewed military action, the Pentagon has continued pressing US defence contractors to accelerate weapons production. Deputy Defense Secretary Steve Feinberg reportedly told industry executives this past week that they have up to 21 days to submit plans for faster delivery schedules and increased output of critical munitions, underscoring concerns within the administration about depleted stockpiles linked to the broader conflict with Iran.

## Why It Matters

The prolonged uncertainty over the Strait of Hormuz, through which a substantial share of the world's seaborne oil trade passes, has kept global crude prices elevated in recent weeks and continues to weigh on sentiment in energy-importing economies, including India. With neither side showing urgency to fully close the gap, markets are likely to stay sensitive to any incremental signals from Washington, Tehran or Muscat in the days ahead.
