The Lok Sabha on Wednesday approved a motion to send the Foreign Contribution (Regulation) Amendment Bill, 2026, to a 31-member Joint Parliamentary Committee for detailed examination, amid loud Opposition protests and demands that the legislation be withdrawn entirely rather than merely reviewed.

## What the motion does

The panel will comprise 21 members from the Lok Sabha and 10 from the Rajya Sabha, with the Lok Sabha members to be nominated by Speaker Om Birla and the Rajya Sabha's by Chairman C P Radhakrishnan. The committee has been directed to submit its report to the Lok Sabha by the last day of the first week of the Winter Session of Parliament later this year. The motion, listed in Wednesday's supplementary business, was moved by Minister of State for Home Affairs Nityanand Rai on behalf of Union Home Minister Amit Shah.

The bill itself was first introduced in the Lok Sabha in late March and proposes tighter government oversight of foreign contributions received by non-governmental organisations operating in India. It also seeks to create a designated authority to manage and dispose of assets belonging to any organisation that loses its FCRA registration.

## Opposition pushes back

Congress MP K C Venugopal criticised the timing of the move, saying the Opposition had only recently been informed that the bill would be referred to committee, and argued that sending it to a panel was not sufficient. "We demand that the Bill be withdrawn," he said, alleging the legislation disproportionately targets NGOs working with minority communities, and further alleged that the Rashtriya Swayamsevak Sangh receives foreign donations without facing similar scrutiny. He also questioned Shah's absence from the House when the motion was moved.

Samajwadi Party chief Akhilesh Yadav and DMK MP T R Baalu both echoed calls for the bill's withdrawal. Parliamentary Affairs Minister Kiren Rijiju rejected the claim that the bill targets any community, telling the House that Opposition parties had themselves previously asked for the legislation to be examined by a House committee, and challenged members to point to any provision that specifically targeted a minority group.

## Wider context

The bill has already drawn attention beyond Parliament. Several chief ministers, including those of Nagaland, Mizoram and Meghalaya, have raised concerns and sought a more detailed review of its provisions, particularly around the impact on Christian-run educational and social welfare institutions that rely on foreign funding. Some US lawmakers have also criticised the proposed changes, a characterisation the Indian government has previously rejected as commentary on an internal legislative matter.

By moving the bill to a Joint Parliamentary Committee rather than pushing it through the House directly, the government has effectively delayed a floor vote until at least the Winter Session, giving both supporters and critics of the legislation a formal venue to press their case. Whether that satisfies Opposition demands for a full withdrawal, or simply shifts the fight to the committee room, is likely to shape how the bill's next phase plays out.
