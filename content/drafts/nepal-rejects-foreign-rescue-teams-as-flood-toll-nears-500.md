Nepal has turned down offers of foreign search-and-rescue assistance following the catastrophic flash floods that tore through Rasuwa district and surrounding areas near the Chinese border, even as the death toll approaches 500 and hundreds of people, including many foreign nationals, remain unaccounted for.

## Domestic agencies leading the operation

A spokesperson for Nepal's foreign ministry said on Friday that the country's own security forces were managing the search-and-rescue effort and did not currently require outside teams on the ground. The statement came despite formal offers of assistance from India, China, the United States, Britain, Japan, South Korea, Sri Lanka and Bangladesh, several of whom have citizens missing in the disaster zone.

South Korea has been among the most vocal in seeking to deploy personnel, given that a number of its nationals working at a hydropower project in the flood-hit region are missing or stranded. Seoul's foreign ministry indicated it would continue discussions with Kathmandu on the matter, while separately pledging financial assistance for relief efforts.

## Why Nepal is holding back

Officials in Kathmandu have been careful to frame the decision as a matter of operational capacity rather than politics, insisting domestic teams are equipped to continue the search. However, the affected Rasuwa district sits close to the Tibet Autonomous Region of China, and some observers have suggested that sensitivities around foreign access to a border-adjacent zone may be a factor in the government's caution. Nepal's foreign ministry has explicitly denied that the decision is connected to concerns about China, saying only that domestic capacity is sufficient for now.

The flood, which struck with little warning, levelled entire settlements, destroyed hydropower infrastructure and swept away roads and bridges in a mountainous region already difficult to access. Rescue teams have had to rely heavily on helicopters given the scale of damage to ground routes, and monsoon weather has periodically hampered air operations.

## International response

While declining physical rescue teams, Nepal has welcomed financial and humanitarian aid from the international community. Washington has pledged funds toward relief efforts, and other governments are expected to channel support through humanitarian organisations rather than deploying personnel directly. The scale of the disaster, one of the deadliest in the region in recent years, has drawn sustained global attention, with China also confirming evacuations on its side of the border as swollen rivers and unstable slopes continued to pose risks well after the initial flooding.

For India, which shares deep cultural and economic ties with Nepal and had also offered rescue assistance, the situation underscores the recurring vulnerability of the Himalayan region to extreme weather events, an issue with implications for cross-border infrastructure, hydropower investment and disaster-preparedness cooperation across South Asia.

Nepali authorities have not ruled out revisiting their position should the search-and-rescue effort prove insufficient in the days ahead, but for now the government appears intent on demonstrating it can manage the response domestically, even as it continues to accept outside financial support to help affected communities rebuild.
