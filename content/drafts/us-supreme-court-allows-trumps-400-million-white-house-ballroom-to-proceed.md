A divided US Supreme Court has allowed construction to continue on a $400 million ballroom project at the White House, handing President Donald Trump a procedural win as litigation over the project's legality continues in lower courts.

## The ruling

The order lifts a lower-court block on the project and replaces a temporary stay that Chief Justice John Roberts had issued shortly before an earlier ruling halting construction was set to take effect. Notably, Roberts himself dissented from the majority's latest decision, aligning with the court's three liberal-leaning justices, who described the construction as "likely unlawful." However, the majority opinion did not rule on the underlying legality of the ballroom project itself. Instead, it found that the preservation group challenging the construction likely lacked the legal standing to bring the case in the first place.

## Background of the dispute

The legal fight was initiated by the National Trust for Historic Preservation, which argued that Trump lacked unilateral authority to carry out the construction, a project that has included the demolition of the White House's East Wing. The group's lawyers accused the administration of racing to finish construction specifically to make the legal challenge moot before courts could rule, a strategy they characterised as trying to "outrun the courts."

A federal district judge ordered a halt to above-ground construction earlier this year, while permitting underground work on bunkers and military installations to continue. That ruling was initially upheld by a divided appeals court panel, with two Democratic-appointed judges determining the matter was one for Congress to decide rather than unilateral executive action.

## The administration's position

Government lawyers have argued that the president holds broad authority to renovate federal buildings, including the White House, and have also invoked national security considerations to justify the project's continuation, a rationale that was not part of the administration's original public explanation when the ballroom was first announced as privately funded.

Officials have said construction crews are working roughly 20 hours a day on the planned 90,000-square-foot structure. Court filings indicate the building's frame is expected to be completed by November, its facade largely finished by April, and the full project completed by August 2028.

## What happens next

While construction can proceed for now, the underlying legal question, whether a president can undertake major structural changes to the White House without congressional approval, remains unresolved and will return to lower courts for further proceedings. Legal observers note that because the project may be substantially complete before a final ruling is reached, the practical stakes of the ongoing litigation could diminish significantly over time, even if courts eventually find the original construction improper.

The case is the latest example of the current administration turning to the Supreme Court's emergency docket to secure quick wins on high-profile disputes, a pattern that has produced a mixed record for the White House once cases receive fuller judicial review.
