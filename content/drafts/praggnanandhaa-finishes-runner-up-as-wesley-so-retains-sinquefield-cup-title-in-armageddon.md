Indian grandmaster R Praggnanandhaa came within a whisker of winning the Sinquefield Cup for the first time early Thursday, finishing tied for first place in the classical standings before losing a tense Armageddon tiebreak to American grandmaster Wesley So, who claimed the title for the third time in his career and the second year running.

## A Tiebreak Decided by Inches

Both players finished the nine-round classical portion of the tournament level on 5.5 points after their final-round games ended in draws — Praggnanandhaa against Anish Giri, and So against Javokhir Sindarov — setting up a playoff to decide the champion. The two rapid games that followed were also drawn, pushing the contest to a single Armageddon game, where the winner takes the title and draw odds decide the outcome if the game itself ends level.

Praggnanandhaa won the draw of lots and chose to play with the white pieces, receiving five minutes on the clock against So's four, though So held draw odds given the shorter time. By his own admission and that of his opponent, Praggnanandhaa had winning chances on the board through large stretches of the game and appeared to be pressing hard, with So later acknowledging that his opponent "was winning in this game." However, without an increment on the clock until deep into the game, Praggnanandhaa said he could not afford to pause long enough to calculate the clearest winning lines, and the game ultimately ended in a draw — enough for So to retain the trophy on tiebreak.

## So's Historic Run

The result marked Wesley So's third Sinquefield Cup title overall and made him the only player to have won the event in consecutive editions. So navigated the tournament with a notably cautious approach in its early rounds, drawing frequently to conserve energy, a strategy he later described as intentional even if it drew some criticism along the way. He finished the event unbeaten across all 12 games in the classical and playoff portions combined.

## A Landmark Tournament for Praggnanandhaa

Despite the runner-up finish, the tournament carried significant milestones for the 21-year-old Indian. His round-eight victory over French grandmaster Maxime Vachier-Lagrave lifted his live rating high enough to make him India's number one ranked player, overtaking compatriot Arjun Erigaisi, and pushed him to world number eight. Praggnanandhaa also secured his place in the 2026 Grand Chess Tour Finals, which begin this Saturday and will also feature So, Fabiano Caruana and Vincent Keymer.

Speaking after the loss, Praggnanandhaa said the defeat stung given how close he came, but that reaching the Finals — where he can still compete for further prize money — was a positive outcome from the week. So, for his part, offered a good-natured consolation to his rival immediately after the game, noting that no player wins every tournament in a season.

## What's Next

The Sinquefield Cup served as the final regular-season leg of the 2026 Grand Chess Tour, with the season-ending Finals now looming this weekend. Praggnanandhaa's return to the Finals, after also competing in the previous edition, sets up another opportunity for the Indian number one to add to his growing list of achievements on the international circuit.
