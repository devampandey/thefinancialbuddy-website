Europe has opened a new front in its effort to regulate how technology companies design their products. On Wednesday, the European Commission presented the **Digital Fairness Act (DFA)**, a proposal aimed at the everyday tricks that apps and websites use to push people into spending more money or time than they intended. Justice and consumer protection Commissioner Michael McGrath said the law would put consumers back in control, and argued that it would make it harder to do business unfairly rather than harder to do business.

## What the proposal would ban

The DFA goes after what regulators call dark patterns: interface designs that steer users towards choices they might not otherwise make. Examples include false claims that a product is almost sold out, countdown timers that create artificial pressure, and checkout flows that make the cheaper option hard to find. The draft combines a general ban on such practices with a list of specific banned tactics.

Other key measures include:

- **Subscriptions:** Every online subscription would need a simple way to cancel, so that ending a plan is as easy as starting one.
- **Addictive design:** Users would be able to switch off features such as endless scrolling and autoplay.
- **Minors:** Loot boxes, the paid random rewards common in video games, would be banned for children, and personalised advertising aimed at minors would be restricted.
- **Influencers:** Creators would have to clearly disclose paid promotions and avoid exploiting the trust of younger followers.
- **Pricing:** More transparency on dynamic pricing, including for concert and sports tickets, where prices can change with demand while a buyer is still in the queue.

## Real penalties this time

The enforcement side may matter as much as the rules. Fines could reach **6% of a company's annual global turnover**. A related update to the EU's consumer protection cooperation rules would let the Commission investigate and fine directly in large cross-border cases, instead of relying only on national authorities. McGrath noted that the current system has never produced a fine.

The DFA would also replace the 2005 Unfair Commercial Practices Directive with a single regulation that applies the same way in every member state. It would cover any business that targets EU consumers, wherever it is based.

## Why Brussels says it is needed

The Commission says more than 80% of popular e-commerce sites and mobile apps use manipulative marketing tactics, and an earlier EU review found that 97% of the most popular websites and apps used at least one dark pattern. It estimates that unfair online practices cost EU consumers about 10.3 billion euros a year. Over a decade, it puts the reduction in consumer harm at about 88.4 billion euros, against compliance costs for businesses of roughly 678 million euros a year.

## Pushback and next steps

Industry groups have warned that the act should not impose disproportionate costs on legitimate businesses. Consumer and finance groups, on the other hand, have broadly welcomed the direction, though some say the dark pattern rules are not ambitious enough for financial services.

The proposal is not yet law. The European Parliament and EU governments must negotiate and agree a final text, a process that usually takes well over a year, and the details could change. Still, the EU's past rules on data privacy and digital markets have often become templates for other countries. Global platforms, gaming companies and subscription businesses, including those based in India that sell into Europe, will be watching the negotiations closely.
