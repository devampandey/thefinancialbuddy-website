British counter-terrorism police on Monday released on bail all five men arrested over an alleged plot near RAF Fairford, while confirming for the first time that detectives are examining whether the suspects were acting, knowingly or not, on behalf of a foreign state. The men were detained early on Sunday near the Gloucestershire base that US bombers have used throughout the war with Iran ([see our earlier coverage](/world/five-arrested-terror-plot-suspicion-raf-fairford-us-bomber-base-england)).

## Who the suspects are

Police have now said the five are all UK nationals aged between 23 and 25, and reports indicate they are from the London area. Their names have not been released, and none has been charged. They remain under investigation on suspicion of explosives offences and of preparing an act of terrorism.

Bail at this stage is notable in a case with this profile. It indicates that police do not yet have enough evidence to bring charges, but it also leaves them free to re-arrest any of the men if forensic work, phone data or financial records turn up something new. Investigators said overnight examination of the scene had produced multiple new lines of inquiry.

## The foreign-state angle

The most significant development came from Laurence Taylor, head of Counter Terrorism Policing, who said the inquiry includes the possibility that the plot was carried out by proxies or by individuals working for a foreign state, whether they knew it or not.

Police did not name a country. The obvious question is Iran. Its Revolutionary Guard has previously described RAF Fairford as a legitimate target because of its role in the air campaign, and British media have reported that investigators are weighing an Iranian connection among other possibilities.

Tehran rejected that quickly. Iran's embassy in London said on Monday that it categorically rejects what it called unfounded and malicious attempts by some British individuals and outlets to tie it to the arrests. It argued such claims only fuel hostility toward Iran in the UK and said it reserves the right to take legal action over damaging coverage.

## A 999 call, not surveillance

New details also emerged about how the men were stopped. A local farmer told the BBC she came across three white vans blocking a road near the base late on Sunday night. When her headlights swung across the scene as she turned around, she saw a group of hooded, masked men who panicked and ran into nearby woods. She called police, and said she was surprised the base did not have tighter surveillance of its own.

Defence Secretary Wes Streeting thanked her for acting quickly but said she had only a partial picture of what happened, and declined to go further on operational grounds. That account sits somewhat awkwardly beside US President Donald Trump's claim on Sunday that American and British authorities had been watching the suspects for a long time.

## What to watch

Two questions will shape the next phase. The first is whether investigators can establish who, if anyone, directed the men; a confirmed state link would carry heavy diplomatic consequences just as Washington and Tehran are exploring a mediated deal. The second is security at British bases hosting US forces, which is likely to face political scrutiny in Parliament after a civilian, rather than base security, raised the alarm.
