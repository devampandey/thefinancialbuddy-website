The Saudi-led coalition fighting in Yemen said on Friday that it had intercepted and destroyed a drone launched by the Houthis over the Makkah region. Coalition spokesman Turki al-Malki announced the interception in a post on X. It is the second time in about six weeks that a Houthi drone has been reported over the province that contains Islam's holiest city.

The coalition gave no details on the drone's intended target or where exactly it was brought down. The Houthis have repeatedly denied that they are targeting Mecca or Medina, and say their strikes are aimed at military and energy sites.

## A second straight day of strikes on the kingdom

The Makkah interception follows a tense Thursday in the Saudi capital. Correspondents for a news agency in Riyadh reported at least eight explosions, and residents of Riyadh, Kharj and the southern city of Najran received phone alerts warning of a hostile aerial threat. The coalition later said its air defences had destroyed two ballistic missiles fired at Riyadh. The warnings were lifted soon afterwards.

That barrage came hours after the Houthis accused the coalition of air strikes on residential areas in western Yemen, which they said killed at least 12 people.

The Houthis have also claimed six separate operations against Saudi targets, naming the Ras Tanura and Rabigh refineries and the international airports in Riyadh and Dammam. Saudi authorities have not confirmed that any of those sites was hit, and the claims could not be independently verified.

## Why the Makkah region matters

Any attack near Mecca carries a symbolic weight that strikes on airports or oil plants do not. In mid-September, Saudi Arabia issued its first security alert for the holy city since 2017 after a drone heading towards it was intercepted. A second incident in the same province suggests that the kingdom's western flank, which has so far drawn less attention than Riyadh and the eastern oil belt, is now part of the conflict.

The economic stakes are high too. Rabigh, on the Red Sea coast north of Jeddah, and Ras Tanura, on the Gulf, are both major refining and export hubs. Even unconfirmed claims of strikes on them add to the risk premium in a crude market already on edge over shipping in the Strait of Hormuz, where Iran says several tankers struck mines this week. The United States disputes that account.

## A truce that no longer exists

Saudi Arabia and the Houthis had kept to a fragile truce for years, but it fell apart over the summer. Since then the two sides have traded attacks almost every day. Earlier Houthi strikes on Riyadh's airport closed it for two weeks and forced the cancellation of high-profile events, a direct hit to the kingdom's efforts to market itself as a destination for tourism and investment.

For India, the escalation matters on two fronts. Saudi Arabia is one of the country's largest crude suppliers, so attacks on its energy facilities feed straight into the import bill. It is also home to a large Indian workforce and receives a steady flow of Indian pilgrims to Mecca and Medina.
