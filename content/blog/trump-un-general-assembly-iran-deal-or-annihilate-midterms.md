US President Donald Trump used his address to the opening day of the United Nations General Assembly's high-level debate to deliver one of his starkest formulations yet on Iran, telling assembled world leaders that he is weighing whether to let Tehran rebuild under a negotiated settlement or to destroy the Islamic Republic outright.

Trump framed the choice as a personal decision still pending, asking whether a deal would be made that allows Iran to rebuild into a far greater country than it had ever been, or whether he would instead "annihilate the Islamic Republic, and do it quickly." He went on to predict that no agreement would materialise until after American voters go to the polls in November, saying he expects a deal shortly after the midterm elections because refusing one would make little sense for Tehran.

## A Defence of the War He Started

Much of the speech was given over to defending the administration's record on the conflicts it has entered. Trump justified the decision to launch military action against Iran earlier this year, presenting it as a necessary corrective rather than a rupture of the international order, and offered a broader defence of how his White House has handled overlapping crises from Ukraine to the Middle East.

The setting gave the remarks added weight. The 81st session convened under the theme of restoring trust and managing transformation, a formulation chosen by a UN establishment increasingly anxious about its own relevance. Secretary-General Antonio Guterres and Assembly President Khalilur Rahman opened proceedings before a hall that will hear from roughly 130 heads of state and government over a debate running through September 26 and resuming on September 28.

## Diplomacy on the Sidelines

The speech was only part of Trump's day in New York. He was scheduled to hold talks with Britain's new prime minister, Andy Burnham, in what would be their first face-to-face meeting since Burnham took office in July, as well as with Japanese Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi. A more informal encounter with Venezuela's acting president, Delcy Rodriguez, was also on the schedule.

Those conversations carry their own agendas. Burnham has signalled he wants to press Washington for additional interceptor missiles for Ukraine and to discuss reopening shipping through the Strait of Hormuz, which has been largely disrupted since the February outbreak of the US-Israeli campaign against Iran. Takaichi has indicated she intends to use Japan's turn at the podium to argue for stronger supply chains, particularly in energy, and for a coordinated international response to rapid advances in artificial intelligence.

## Why It Matters for India

For Indian readers, the immediate transmission channel is energy. Brent crude has been trading around the 100-dollar mark, easing roughly 7 percent over the previous five sessions before ticking higher again on Tuesday, and the Hormuz disruption remains the single largest variable in that price. India imports the overwhelming majority of the crude it consumes, which means the gap between a negotiated settlement and a wider war is not an abstraction for Indian households or for the current account.

A deal deferred until after November, as Trump suggested, would imply several more months of exactly the uncertainty that has kept oil elevated and shipping insurance costly. Markets, for now, appear to be pricing neither outcome with much conviction.
