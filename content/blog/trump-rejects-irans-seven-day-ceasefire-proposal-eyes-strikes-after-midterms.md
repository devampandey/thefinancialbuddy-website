President Donald Trump has rejected Iran's latest offer to de-escalate their ongoing conflict, and has told aides he expects military strikes on Iran to resume once November's midterm elections are out of the way, according to a Wall Street Journal report picked up by Reuters and multiple international outlets early Saturday.

## What Iran had offered

The rejected proposal came from Iranian Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi, who laid out a compressed seven-day roadmap at the United Nations General Assembly on Friday. Under the plan, fighting would halt across all fronts, including Lebanon, within the first week, while the United States would lift its naval blockade and oil sanctions and release frozen Iranian assets. The Strait of Hormuz, a critical global oil shipping route that has been repeatedly disrupted through the conflict, would reopen on the seventh day, with nuclear program talks to follow only after that. Qatar has been facilitating indirect contact between Washington and Tehran, while Secretary of State Marco Rubio has publicly cautioned against describing recent contacts as a breakthrough.

## Trump's response, as described by officials

Officials cited by the Journal said Trump communicated to his team that he does not intend to accept the seven-day framework and instead expects bombing operations against Iran to pick back up once the midterm elections have concluded. At the same time, those officials characterized Trump as reluctant to greenlight a major new round of combat immediately, suggesting the scope and timing of any renewed strikes remain undecided internally. His ultimate position, they added, could still shift depending on how the midterms play out or how the situation on the ground evolves in the coming weeks.

Reuters, which relayed the Journal's reporting, noted it had not independently verified the account, underscoring that the report rests on unnamed officials' characterization of private conversations rather than an on-record statement from the White House or Trump himself.

## Why the timing matters

The rejection lands at a delicate moment. The Iran-US conflict is approaching its seventh month, and Tehran's latest offer notably reordered the sequence from an earlier, since-collapsed June memorandum of understanding, apparently in an attempt to make the terms more palatable to Washington by shortening timelines. Markets and shipping insurers have been watching Hormuz-related developments closely all year, given the strait's role in global crude flows, and any signal that talks have stalled again tends to feed back into oil price volatility.

For now, the disclosure suggests Washington is treating the midterms as a political factor shaping the tempo of the conflict, even as diplomats keep searching for an opening. Whether Iran responds to the rejection with a revised offer, or reverts to a harder line, is likely to shape the next phase of an already prolonged standoff.
