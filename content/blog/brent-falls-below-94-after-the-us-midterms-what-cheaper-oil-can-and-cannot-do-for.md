Brent crude traded near $93.6 a barrel early on Thursday, November 5, its lowest level in about two months, after falling almost 8% on Wednesday. Traders are betting that the Democratic Party's capture of both chambers of the US Congress will push President Donald Trump toward a negotiated end to the war with Iran. For India, which buys most of its crude from abroad, a drop of this size matters more than almost any other market move this year. It also comes with conditions that are easy to miss in a relief rally.

## How far and how fast oil has fallen

The scale is unusual even by the standards of a volatile year. Brent peaked near $118 last Friday, settled at $103.60 on Tuesday, and on Wednesday traded below $100 for the first time since early October. By Thursday morning in Asia, Brent futures were down another 99 cents at about $93.63, and US benchmark WTI was near $87.13. That leaves Brent roughly 20% below where it stood six days ago.

The trigger was political rather than physical. Vice President JD Vance publicly called the Iran war the reason Republicans lost, and Trump told the Financial Times that the US has no immediate plans to strike Iran. Qatar has stepped up its mediation. Markets read all of this as a sign that the White House now has less room, and less appetite, to escalate.

## Why the physical market is less convinced

Two details argue for caution. First, Murban crude, a physical Gulf grade, was at about $100.90 on Thursday, a premium of roughly $7 over Brent futures. When barrels that can actually be loaded cost that much more than the paper benchmark, it suggests supply from the Gulf is still tight, even as financial traders price in peace.

Second, the conflict has not paused. Iran's Revolutionary Guards said on Thursday that two oil tankers and a tugboat caught fire in separate incidents in the Strait of Hormuz after trying to use routes Tehran has not approved; one tanker reportedly hit a mine. These claims have not been independently confirmed. Iranian officials also say the two sides remain far from a deal, and US officials have told reporters the military is still preparing for possible major combat. Oil has priced in an exit that has not yet happened.

## The India transmission: rupee first

The fastest channel is the currency. The rupee settled at 94.64 per dollar on Wednesday, and currency dealers expected it to open stronger, around 94.35 to 94.40, on Thursday. Lower oil reduces the daily dollar demand of refiners, which has been one of the steadiest sources of pressure on the rupee since the war began on February 28. The RBI had already moved state oil marketers' dollar purchases into a separate window to keep that demand out of the interbank market, so a cheaper import bill now compounds an existing policy cushion.

Foreign investors are also returning. Overseas funds bought Indian shares worth about Rs 7,284 crore on Wednesday, their fourth straight day of net buying, and the Nifty 50 and Sensex have risen about 5.6% over five sessions, their longest winning run in nearly five months. Cheaper oil improves India's inflation and current account outlook at the same time, which is the combination foreign portfolio investors look for.

## Inflation and the pump price question

On inflation, the RBI's long-standing rule of thumb is that a 10% change in crude prices moves headline inflation by roughly 20 basis points, all else equal. A sustained 20% fall would therefore be meaningful for the inflation outlook ahead of the RBI's next policy meeting. The key word is sustained: one week of falling prices does not change a central bank's forecast.

Households should not expect quick relief at the pump. Retail petrol and diesel prices have been frozen since the second half of May, and state-run marketers spent months selling fuel below cost. Cheaper crude is more likely to repair their balance sheets first than to show up as a price cut. Indian Oil itself warned last week that falling crude could cause inventory losses in coming quarters, because oil bought at higher prices loses value on paper when prices drop ([see our report on IOC's results](/business/indian-oil-posts-record-rs-21-630-crore-q2-profit-as-refining-margins-outrun-a-rs)).

## What this means

Our view is that this is a genuine improvement for India's macro picture, but one that depends on a diplomatic outcome markets have assumed rather than seen.

The clearest beneficiaries if prices hold below $100 are the rupee, government finances, and oil-consuming sectors such as airlines, paints, tyres and chemicals, where crude-linked inputs are a large share of costs. Oil marketing companies gain on retail margins but face inventory write-downs, so their near-term earnings could look worse before they look better. Upstream producers such as ONGC and Oil India give up some of the windfall they have enjoyed.

The trade-off for the government is between passing relief to consumers and letting state refiners recover losses. Given the scale of under-recoveries on fuel and LPG this year, we expect the second to take priority, at least for several weeks.

India also holds a small edge that other importers do not. An Iranian lawmaker said this week that Indian ships are exempt from Tehran's transit restrictions in Hormuz, though fees still apply. That helps physical supply, but it is a political concession that can be withdrawn.

What remains unclear is whether Washington and Tehran actually return to talks, and whether Hormuz traffic recovers. Watch three things over the next fortnight: the gap between Murban and Brent, which shows whether physical supply is easing; any confirmed restart of direct US-Iran talks; and whether the rupee can hold its gains without RBI support. If those line up, sub-$100 oil can become a base case rather than a bounce. If talks stall or more tankers are hit, this week's drop could reverse as quickly as it came.
