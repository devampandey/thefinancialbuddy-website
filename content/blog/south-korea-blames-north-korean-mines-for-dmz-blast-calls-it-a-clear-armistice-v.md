South Korea has formally pinned last week's landmine explosion inside the Demilitarized Zone (DMZ) on North Korea. Defence Minister Kang Shin-chul told a parliamentary defence committee on Tuesday that the blast was a clear violation of the 1953 armistice that halted the Korean War, and that Seoul would respond.

Kang said responsibility lay with Pyongyang and that the military had a range of options it would roll out in stages. He declined to say what those steps would be.

## What Investigators Found

The explosion on September 21 took place south of the Military Demarcation Line (MDL), the line that splits the DMZ between the two Koreas, and injured South Korean soldiers. News agencies have given slightly different casualty counts, with AFP and Yonhap reporting three injured and one losing a foot, while Reuters cited two injured personnel.

On Monday, the South Korean military had said North Korean mines were highly likely to be the cause. A joint team of South Korean officers and the United Nations Command's Military Armistice Commission has been examining the site since Sunday.

That probe turned up fresh evidence on Tuesday. The Joint Chiefs of Staff told lawmakers that another North Korean anti-personnel mine, made of plastic resin, had been found near the blast site. The military said this device differed from the mines believed to have caused the explosion.

Kwon Dae-won, vice chairman of the Joint Chiefs, said the mines did not appear to have washed or drifted into the area, and that the UN Command had assessed they were located clearly on the southern side of the MDL.

## The Key Unanswered Question

Kang told lawmakers the mines appeared to have been laid as part of North Korea's ongoing work to fortify its side of the border. The issue Seoul is now weighing, he said, is intent: whether North Korean forces placed the mines knowing that South Korean troops could walk into the area and be hurt.

That distinction will likely shape how forceful South Korea's response is. North Korea's state media has not commented on the incident.

## Echoes of 2015

The episode recalls a 2015 incident in which North Korean mines wounded South Korean soldiers near the border. Seoul responded then by switching its anti-North Korea propaganda loudspeakers back on along the DMZ, which set off a tense military standoff that only ended after high-level talks between the two sides.

The DMZ is formally demilitarised, but the strips of land on either side are among the most heavily fortified and mined frontiers in the world, and the two Koreas remain technically at war because the conflict ended in a truce rather than a peace treaty.

## Awkward Timing for Seoul

The blast complicates President Lee Jae Myung's effort to coax North Korea back to talks. Pyongyang has in recent years labelled South Korea a hostile state and dropped its long-held goal of peaceful reunification, leaving little room for dialogue.

A staged response gives Seoul room to signal resolve without closing the door entirely. How far it goes may depend on what the joint investigation concludes about whether the mines were meant to harm South Korean troops, and on whether Pyongyang chooses to respond at all.
