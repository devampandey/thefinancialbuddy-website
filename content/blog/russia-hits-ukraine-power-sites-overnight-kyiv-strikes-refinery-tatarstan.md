Russia and Ukraine traded fresh strikes on each other's energy infrastructure overnight into Sunday, underscoring how the war's focus has increasingly shifted to a contest over power grids and fuel supply as both sides look to grind down the other's capacity ahead of winter.

Russian forces struck power facilities and other targets across southern Ukraine overnight, according to Ukrainian officials, part of a pattern of attacks on the country's energy grid that Kyiv has warned could make this winter among the hardest since the war began in 2022. Separately, in the northeastern Kharkiv region, at least five people were injured after Russian drone strikes hit civilian areas, local authorities said.

Ukraine's forces responded with a long-range strike deep inside Russian territory. Ukrainian military and intelligence units said they carried out a joint operation targeting the Slavyansk-EKO oil refinery in Slavyansk-on-Kuban in Russia's Krasnodar region, sparking a fire at the facility early Sunday. Drones also struck a petrochemical hub in Nizhnekamsk, in Russia's Tatarstan region, further from the front line than many of Ukraine's earlier refinery strikes this year.

The exchange fits a broader pattern that has defined recent months of the conflict: Ukraine has been running a sustained campaign against Russian oil refineries and fuel logistics, aiming to raise the economic cost of the war for Moscow, while Russia has stepped up attacks on Ukraine's power stations and grid infrastructure. Energy analysts have noted that Ukraine's refinery strikes have already measurably dented Russia's domestic refining capacity in recent weeks, forcing some fuel processing to be redirected abroad.

The overnight strikes came a day after Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelensky said Russia had launched close to 500 strike drones at the country in a single day, one of the heavier drone barrages of the year, adding to pressure on Ukraine's air defences and repair crews.

The renewed exchanges also come amid unresolved questions over when, or whether, both sides might return to the negotiating table. US envoys Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner travelled to both Moscow and Kyiv for high-level talks in the past week, but no clear timeline for follow-up negotiations has emerged, and fighting on the ground and in the air has shown little sign of easing in the meantime.

For now, both capitals appear focused on demonstrating they can keep inflicting damage on the other's energy backbone rather than on any imminent diplomatic breakthrough. Ukrainian officials have repeatedly said that degrading Russia's refining and export capacity is central to their strategy of pressuring Moscow economically, while Russian strikes on Ukraine's grid are widely seen as an attempt to weaken civilian morale and infrastructure resilience before the cold months set in.

Neither Moscow nor Kyiv has provided a full, independently verified damage assessment for Sunday's strikes, and casualty figures on both sides typically take time to be confirmed. The scale and location of the strikes nonetheless mark one of the more geographically extensive exchanges of the past week, reaching from Ukraine's south to Russia's Volga region.
