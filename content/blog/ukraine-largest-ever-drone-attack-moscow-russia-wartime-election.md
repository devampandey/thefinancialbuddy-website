Russia closed the third and final day of its parliamentary election on Sunday under the shadow of what its own capital's mayor described as the largest drone attack Moscow has ever faced, in an escalation that underlines how tightly the war in Ukraine and Kremlin politics are now intertwined.

Ukrainian forces launched more than 1,000 drones at targets across Russia overnight, with several hundred of them aimed at the Moscow region, according to Russian officials. Moscow's Mayor Sergei Sobyanin said air defences intercepted around 450 drones headed for the capital but that the barrage still struck the Moscow oil refinery and damaged a residential building. Russian authorities said the wider assault, which also reportedly involved missiles, was tracked over Crimea and the Black Sea, where Russia's defence ministry claimed to have brought down more than 1,100 Ukrainian drones nationwide.

The regional governor overseeing the greater Moscow area said the strikes killed two people, a 74-year-old man and a 44-year-old woman, and left 20 others injured, including three children. President Volodymyr Zelenskyy acknowledged the operation on social media, saying Ukrainian forces had used a mix of missiles and drones, including domestically built Flamingo and Pelican systems, to hit oil and logistics infrastructure that helps fund Russia's war effort.

## A Vote With No Real Opposition

The attack landed on the closing day of Russia's first parliamentary election held since its full-scale invasion of Ukraine began more than four and a half years ago. The vote has drawn criticism from Kyiv and Western governments for taking place, in part, in four Ukrainian regions that Moscow illegally annexed but does not fully control, as well as in Crimea, annexed in 2014. Independent monitors and opposition voices have been almost entirely absent from the race, and the outcome is widely expected to reinforce President Vladimir Putin's grip on the legislature.

Sobyanin framed the overnight strikes as a deliberate attempt to rattle voters and disrupt the final hours of balloting, telling reporters the attack was unprecedented in scale and calling it a failed bid to influence the election. Kyiv has not directly linked the timing of its strike to the vote, but the scale of the barrage, and its focus on the Russian capital rather than front-line military targets, was itself a signal that Ukraine wanted the attack to register far beyond the battlefield.

## Part Of A Widening Long-Range Campaign

The Moscow strikes come as both sides lean more heavily on long-range weapons while the roughly 1,250-kilometre front line in eastern and southern Ukraine has largely stalled, slowed by dense drone coverage and ground robots that make troop movement difficult. Ukraine has increasingly built its own long-range drone and missile programmes over the course of the war, using them to pressure Russia's energy and logistics networks in a strategy aimed at raising the war's economic cost for ordinary Russians and, Kyiv hopes, pushing Putin toward genuine negotiations.

For now, there is little sign of that shift. Putin has given no indication he intends to wind down the invasion, even as Ukraine's strike capability grows more sophisticated and its reach deeper into Russian territory. With the election result all but assured, the attack on Moscow is likely to be remembered less for its effect on the vote and more as a marker of how far the aerial war between the two countries has escalated.
