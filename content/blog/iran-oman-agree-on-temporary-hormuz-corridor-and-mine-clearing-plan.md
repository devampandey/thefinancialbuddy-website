Iran and Oman have taken a significant step toward easing the months-long shipping crisis in the Strait of Hormuz, with the two countries' top diplomats agreeing on a framework for a temporary transit corridor and a joint mine-clearing operation in the strategic waterway.

Omani Foreign Minister Badr Albusaidi travelled to Tehran on Tuesday for talks with his Iranian counterpart, Abbas Araghchi, focused on the future of navigation through the strait, which both nations border. In a joint statement, the two sides said they had discussed "a phased framework that could provide a practical and implementable basis for moving forward," centred on establishing a temporary joint navigation corridor and clearing the strait of mines.

## What Was Agreed

According to the statement, technical negotiations between Tehran and Muscat will continue with the goal of eventually reaching a permanent navigation corridor and a broader arrangement for managing the strait, including systems for exchanging information, coordinating vessel traffic, and providing navigational and security services. Albusaidi said afterward that he hoped the countries would "soon announce" the temporary corridor, adding that a permanent solution would "follow in due course."

Both ministers stressed that any eventual arrangement must respect the sovereignty of their respective countries, and said they intend to hold joint discussions with other Gulf coastal states to ensure any deal complies with international law.

## Backdrop of a Paralysed Waterway

The talks come against the backdrop of the wider Middle East war that broke out after the United States and Israel struck Iran in late February, prompting Tehran to blockade the Strait of Hormuz and Washington to impose a naval counter-blockade on Iranian ports. Traffic through the waterway, which under normal conditions carries roughly a fifth of the world's seaborne oil and liquefied natural gas, has remained largely paralysed since.

The diplomatic movement followed a separate announcement from US President Donald Trump, who said on social media that "all mines have been removed and/or detonated" from international waters in the strait, citing the US Navy, while warning that any new mines laid by Iran would be "immediately and systematically destroyed." Underlining that the situation remains fragile, an oil tanker was reportedly struck by an unidentified projectile and disabled Tuesday near the entrance to the strait off Oman's coast, according to the United Kingdom Maritime Trade Operations agency.

Iranian officials have continued to insist that a full reopening of the strait depends on Washington meeting Tehran's broader demands, including lifting its naval blockade, dropping oil-related sanctions, and unfreezing Iranian assets held abroad. Those demands come even as the United States has moved to tighten economic pressure on Iran, having expanded secondary sanctions earlier this week targeting countries and entities that continue business ties with Tehran.

## A Cautious Signal Amid Ongoing Tension

Despite that pressure campaign, there are early signs Washington sees reduced near-term risk of the conflict widening. US officials have reportedly begun returning personnel to some diplomatic missions in the Middle East that were evacuated or scaled back earlier in the war, even as several outposts are expected to initially operate below full capacity.

For now, the Tehran talks mark the clearest sign yet that Iran and Oman are converging on a workable, if temporary, mechanism to unclog one of the world's most important energy chokepoints — even as the broader terms for ending the standoff remain unresolved.
