Saudi Arabia's Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman placed two calls to US President Donald Trump on Thursday urging Washington to launch direct military strikes against Yemen's Houthi movement, as the Iran-aligned group pushed toward a strategically vital Red Sea chokepoint. According to US officials familiar with the calls, Trump turned down the request both times, with the administration signalling it has no immediate plans to intervene militarily against the Houthis.

## Why It Matters

The urgency behind the Crown Prince's outreach stems from rapid Houthi advances along Yemen's western coast. Oil prices jumped sharply on Thursday after Houthi forces seized a strategically important coastal city, bringing them closer to the Bab al-Mandab Strait, one of the world's busiest shipping corridors and a critical route for Saudi oil exports. With Iran already disrupting traffic through the Strait of Hormuz further east, a Houthi foothold at Bab al-Mandab would hand Tehran a second major lever of pressure over the United States and its Gulf partners, compounding an already volatile energy and security picture in the region.

## What's Driving The Escalation

Tensions between Saudi Arabia and the Houthis reignited earlier this year after Riyadh blocked an Iranian aircraft carrying senior Houthi officials from landing in Sanaa. The Houthis responded by targeting Saudi tankers moving through the Red Sea and later widened their campaign to include Saudi energy infrastructure, prompting Saudi-backed Yemeni forces to respond with airstrikes and ground operations. In a notable shift from earlier this year, when Riyadh indicated it could manage the Houthi threat independently, the kingdom has increasingly sought deeper American backing as the fighting has intensified and Saudi-aligned forces have been pushed back on the ground.

## Washington's Calculus

Rather than committing US forces to a direct confrontation, the Trump administration has opted to provide Saudi Arabia with intelligence support and targeting information on Houthi positions. Roughly 200 American military personnel are already stationed in Saudi Arabia in a non-combat support capacity. Officials say Washington's priority remains keeping US forces concentrated on Iran and the Strait of Hormuz, avoiding the opening of an additional military front even as it deepens cooperation with Riyadh. A senior administration official framed the approach as protecting core American interests, particularly freedom of navigation through the Red Sea, while leaving the primary responsibility for confronting the Houthis to regional partners.

Adm. Brad Cooper, the head of US Central Command, travelled to Saudi Arabia on Thursday for urgent coordination talks, underscoring the level of concern within Washington even as it holds off on direct action.

## The Broader Picture

The episode illustrates the delicate balancing act facing the US as multiple regional flashpoints demand attention simultaneously. For Saudi Arabia, the request for American strikes marks a striking departure from its posture in prior months and reflects growing alarm over the pace of Houthi gains. For the United States, the response reflects a preference for calibrated, indirect support over direct military engagement, at least for now, even as global oil markets react to the mounting instability along one of the world's most consequential trade routes.
