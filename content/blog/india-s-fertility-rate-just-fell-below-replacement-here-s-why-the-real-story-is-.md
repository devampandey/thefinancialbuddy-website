I was reading an old argument from investor Ruchir Sharma a while back, built around a simple question he says matters more for a country's growth prospects than almost anything else: is the talent pool growing? His point, made about a decade ago, was that the weak global recovery after 2008 had less to do with debt or demand than with a quieter supply-side problem — shrinking growth in the working-age population, the group of people between 15 and 64 who actually do the producing. I think that question is worth asking about India right now, because a number just crossed a threshold that sounds alarming and, on its own, mostly isn't.

The Headline Number

India's total fertility rate has fallen to 1.9 children per woman, below the replacement level of 2.1, according to both the UNFPA's 2025 State of World Population report and the government's own Sample Registration System data released this year. This is the first time India has measured below replacement nationally. It's a real milestone, and it's being treated as one — a sign that India is following the same path as China, Japan and much of Europe into long-run population decline.

Here's what that framing leaves out. A falling fertility rate doesn't shrink the workforce the day it crosses a threshold; it takes fifteen to twenty years for fewer births to show up as fewer new workers, because the people who'll be 20-year-olds in 2046 are the ones being born now. In the meantime, India's working-age share of the population is still climbing — up to 68.6% in 2026 from 66.0% a decade ago — and most demographic projections have that share continuing to rise until somewhere around 2041, when India's dividend is expected to peak and then start reversing. The talent pool, in Sharma's terms, is still growing. It just won't be for much longer than most people assume.

Why the National Number Is Hiding the Real Story

What I think gets lost in a single national TFR figure is how little India behaves like one demographic unit. Kerala and Tamil Nadu are both sitting at a fertility rate of roughly 1.3, and Delhi is lower still at 1.2 — numbers that put them closer to Japan or South Korea than to the rest of India. Bihar, by contrast, is still at 2.9, comfortably above replacement. That's not a small gap. It means the "is the talent pool growing" question doesn't have one answer for India — it has at least two, running on completely different clocks. The southern states are already further down the path toward an aging, shrinking workforce that the national momentum math is currently masking. The northern and eastern states are the ones actually supplying the growth in India's working-age numbers over the next two decades. Any company, investor or policymaker treating "India's demographic dividend" as a single, uniform tailwind is missing which parts of the country are actually generating it.

The Lever That Matters More Than the Birth Rate

Sharma's framework has a second half that I think is more useful for India today than the first: alongside looking at projected growth in the working-age population, look at what a country is actually doing to counteract a slowdown — either encouraging more births, which he notes has a consistently poor track record worldwide, or drawing more of the existing adult population into the active workforce. For India, that second lever is sitting in plain sight. Female labour force participation stood at 40% in the latest annual Periodic Labour Force Survey data, with urban participation lagging even further behind at under 28%. That is a huge pool of working-age women who are already here, already counted in the population, and not counted in the workforce. Closing even part of that gap would add to India's effective talent pool faster, and more cheaply, than any plausible change in the birth rate ever could.

What I'd Actually Watch

I don't think the fertility rate crossing 2.1 is the number to watch over the next few years. The working-age share is still rising and will keep doing so for roughly another decade and a half regardless of what happens to births today. What I'd watch instead are the two things that determine whether India actually converts that remaining window into growth: whether female labour force participation keeps climbing off a 40% base, and whether the gap between a Kerala-at-1.3 and a Bihar-at-2.9 India starts converging or keeps widening. Those two numbers, not the national TFR headline, are the ones that will tell us whether India's talent pool is being used, not just whether it's growing.
