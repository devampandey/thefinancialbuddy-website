The Lok Sabha on August 3 passed a bill to raise the sanctioned strength of the Supreme Court of India from 34 to 38 judges, including the Chief Justice of India. The legislation cleared the lower house with little debate, as opposition members continued protests inside the chamber over unrelated issues, including police action against student demonstrators at Jantar Mantar and allegations concerning the Ram Temple in Ayodhya.

## What the Bill Does

The Supreme Court (Number of Judges) Amendment Bill, 2026 amends the Supreme Court (Number of Judges) Act, 1956, lifting the count of puisne judges from 33 to 37. Together with the Chief Justice of India, the court's total sanctioned strength rises to 38. The government had already implemented this expansion through an ordinance route earlier in the year — the Supreme Court (Number of Judges) Amendment Ordinance, 2026, which took effect on May 16, 2026. Tuesday's bill formally repeals that ordinance and writes the higher judge count into the permanent statute, a standard procedural step required to keep an ordinance's provisions in force beyond a limited window.

## Why the Government Says It's Needed

Introducing the bill, the government cited a mounting backlog of cases as the central justification. Official figures put the number of pending matters before the Supreme Court at just over 92,000 as of January 1, 2026. Additional judges, the government argued, would help the court hear more matters in parallel and chip away at delays that have built up over several years, particularly in special leave petitions and constitutional benches that require multiple judges to sit together.

This is not the first time Parliament has expanded the Supreme Court's bench strength. The court's sanctioned strength has been increased periodically since independence — moving from an original complement of eight judges in 1950 through several enlargements as the volume of litigation grew alongside the country's population and economy. Tuesday's move continues that trend, though it comes at a moment of heightened friction between the ruling party and opposition benches over other matters entirely.

## The Political Backdrop

The manner of the bill's passage drew some attention in itself. With opposition MPs occupied in protests inside the House, the legislation moved through with minimal floor discussion — a pattern that has recurred for several bills during the ongoing session. Opposition parties have used the session to press the government on the Jantar Mantar police action and the Ayodhya temple allegations, leaving less parliamentary time devoted to scrutinizing legislative business such as this judicial strength expansion.

The bill will now need to clear the Rajya Sabha before receiving presidential assent to become law. Given that the underlying expansion has already been operational since May via ordinance, the Rajya Sabha's passage is expected to be a formality that regularizes an arrangement already in effect.

## What It Means Going Forward

A larger bench strength alone does not guarantee faster case disposal — new judges take time to be appointed through the collegium process, and courtroom infrastructure and support staff must scale alongside judicial numbers. Legal observers will be watching how quickly the Supreme Court collegium moves to recommend appointments to fill the four additional vacancies created by this amendment, and whether the expanded strength translates into a measurable dent in the pendency figures cited to justify the change.
