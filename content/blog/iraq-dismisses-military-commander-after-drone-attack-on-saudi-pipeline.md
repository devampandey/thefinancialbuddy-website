Iraq's Prime Minister Ali al-Zaidi dismissed a senior military commander early Saturday after an investigation confirmed that a recent drone attack on a critical Saudi Arabian oil pipeline had been launched from Iraqi territory, according to a statement from his office. The move follows days of diplomatic strain between Baghdad and Riyadh over strikes that damaged one of Saudi Arabia's most strategically important pieces of energy infrastructure.

## What Happened To The Pipeline

Saudi Arabia's East-West Pipeline, which carries crude oil from the kingdom's eastern fields to the Red Sea port of Yanbu, was struck by several drones on Thursday in the Riyadh and Madina regions. The Saudi Energy Ministry said the attack caused injuries and forced a temporary shutdown of the line, while the Saudi Foreign Ministry said Friday that its investigation traced the drones back to Iraqi territory. Riyadh condemned the strike but said it would refrain from retaliating for now, at the request of Baghdad, while reserving the right to take "all appropriate action" to protect its security and facilities.

The pipeline carries outsized strategic weight at the moment. With shipping through the Strait of Hormuz disrupted for months by Iran's ongoing conflict with the United States and Israel, Saudi Arabia has increasingly leaned on the Red Sea route via Yanbu as an alternative corridor for its oil exports. An attack on that pipeline therefore threatens one of the kingdom's few remaining workarounds to the Hormuz bottleneck.

## Baghdad's Response

According to the Iraqi prime minister's office, investigators determined that the drones were launched from a site within Maysan province in southern Iraq. Al-Zaidi ordered the immediate removal of the province's Operations Commander and directed the formation of an investigative council to examine the Maysan Operations Command further. Iraqi officials also said the prime minister is weighing legal action against those responsible, stressing that Baghdad would not tolerate activity that endangers its relations with neighbouring states.

In public statements, Iraq condemned the attack and reaffirmed that its territory and airspace "will not be a launching point for aggression against any country." Baghdad separately thanked Saudi Arabia for holding back its response while the investigation ran its course.

## A Wider Regional Flashpoint

The pipeline strike lands amid a rapidly deteriorating security picture across the Gulf and the Red Sea. In recent days, Iran-aligned Houthi rebels in Yemen have seized the port city of Mokha and the strategically vital Perim Island, effectively bringing the entire Bab el-Mandeb Strait under their control. That waterway links the Red Sea to the Gulf of Aden and carries roughly a tenth of global seaborne trade, and its capture by the Houthis compounds fears that two of the world's most important oil chokepoints — Hormuz and Bab el-Mandeb — could both fall under the influence of Iran-aligned forces at the same time.

Analysts following the conflict say the timing of the pipeline attack, coming so soon after the Houthi advance, raises questions about coordination among Iran-backed groups even as Tehran and Baghdad publicly distance themselves from the incident. For now, both Riyadh and Baghdad appear to be trying to contain the fallout diplomatically rather than let it spiral into a broader confrontation, but the dismissal of a senior Iraqi commander underscores how seriously Baghdad is treating the breach.

The developments come as global energy markets remain on edge over the possibility of further disruption to Gulf oil flows, with traders watching closely for any sign that either chokepoint crisis could escalate further in the days ahead.
