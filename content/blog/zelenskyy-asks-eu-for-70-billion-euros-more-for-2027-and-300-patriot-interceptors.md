Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy used Friday's EU leaders' summit in Brussels to put a price on the next year of war: 155 billion euros. Of that, he said Kyiv needs at least 70 billion euros in additional money from European partners in 2027, over and above the 90 billion euro loan the bloc has already agreed to cover Ukraine's needs for 2026 and 2027 combined.

He called the figure "realistic" and framed it as an investment in holding Russia back rather than a rescue. The request lands at an awkward moment for European capitals already wrestling with rising borrowing costs and a bitter fight over the bloc's next long-term budget.

## Three asks, not one

The funding request was only one of three. Zelenskyy also called for a winter package of 300 Patriot interceptors aimed specifically at Russian ballistic missiles, which have been hammering Ukraine's energy grid. Patriots remain the main Western system capable of reliably stopping such missiles, but the European countries that still hold stocks have their own reasons to hesitate about giving more away.

His third ask concerned the roughly 200 billion euros of Russian central bank assets immobilised inside the EU. Zelenskyy proposed placing them under a special EU-controlled custodian to reduce the risk that they are ever unfrozen and handed back as part of a deal that excludes Kyiv. Belgium, which holds the bulk of those assets, has repeatedly refused to touch them before a peace settlement, and Prime Minister Bart De Wever restated that position overnight. Any decision to release the assets requires unanimity among member states.

## Where the money would go

Zelenskyy said much of the extra money would fund drones aimed at Russian forces, supply chains and the wider war economy. He argued that Ukraine could produce two to three times more drones, depending on the type, if it had the cash. He also urged leaders to invest in joint weapons production both in Ukraine and in their own countries.

The size of the gap is not entirely new. Kyiv had already flagged a shortfall of about 70 billion euros for next year, and Brussels plans to disburse around 45 billion euros under the existing loan in 2027. The working assumption had been that non-EU partners would help cover the rest, but so far Norway is the only one to have committed significant new money. Austria's chancellor, Christian Stocker, used the summit to complain that Ukraine's financial needs have been communicated unclearly.

## The Trump backdrop

The summit took place amid an open row between Kyiv and Washington. President Donald Trump has called Ukraine's strikes on Russian oil refineries a disgrace and said he finds Zelenskyy harder to deal with than Vladimir Putin. Zelenskyy, in turn, warned that a recent US-Russia arrangement easing restrictions on Russian diesel could be followed by relief on gas, banking and other sectors.

European leaders offered warm words but no new money or weapons on the day. One concrete pledge did emerge: Belgium said it would deliver 10 F-16 fighter jets to Ukraine before the end of the year.

## Why it matters

The 70 billion euro figure sets up the next big fight inside the EU. Joint borrowing is politically difficult, the frozen-assets route is blocked by Belgium, and bilateral contributions would fall on a handful of wealthier states. How Europe answers will shape not only Ukraine's battlefield capacity next year but also energy and defence spending across the continent, with knock-on effects for global oil and gas markets that India watches closely.
