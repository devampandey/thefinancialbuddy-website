The United States military carried out fresh strikes on targets linked to Iran's Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps (IRGC) on Tuesday, reigniting a cycle of confrontation that had been largely dormant for roughly a month. US Central Command said the action followed recent attempted attacks by Iranian forces on commercial shipping moving through the Strait of Hormuz, as well as threats against American personnel stationed in the region.

## What triggered the latest escalation

The renewed fighting traces back to the weekend, when US forces struck rocket launcher positions on Larak Island, an Iranian-controlled point overlooking the strait, after intelligence suggested the IRGC was preparing to lay mines in the shipping channel. Iran responded by firing missiles toward American positions in Jordan; officials said air defence systems intercepted all but one projectile, which was allowed to pass through after being assessed as no threat. The United Arab Emirates separately reported intercepting a drone launched from Iranian territory over its waters.

President Trump had signalled over the weekend that further retaliation was likely, telling reporters the US would "hit them hard" if provoked again. Tuesday's strikes appear to be the follow-through on that warning, with CENTCOM describing the campaign as a targeted response rather than a broader offensive.

## Oil markets react

Energy markets moved quickly on the news. Crude prices, which had already been climbing through Monday on fears of a wider Gulf confrontation, extended gains as traders priced in the risk of disruption to one of the world's most important oil transit corridors. Roughly a fifth of global oil consumption passes through the Strait of Hormuz, making any sustained conflict there a direct threat to global energy supply chains.

Iranian officials, for their part, have downplayed the impact on their own exports. Speaking before the latest strikes, the head of Iran's national oil company insisted operations at export terminals had not been interrupted despite mounting military pressure.

## A fragile ceasefire framework unravels further

The exchange of fire comes months after Washington and Tehran had entered into negotiations built around a memorandum of understanding intended to wind down hostilities. Under that arrangement, Iran had agreed to keep the strait clear of mines in exchange for an eased naval posture and sanctions relief from the US side. That framework has frayed considerably in recent weeks, with both sides trading blame over who broke the truce first.

Diplomats from the region have continued to signal openness to returning to the negotiating table. Iranian leadership has said it would resume compliance with the memorandum if Washington did the same, though hardline elements within the IRGC have pushed for a harder line, including threats to expand strikes toward Gulf states seen as aligned with US interests.

## Why it matters beyond the region

For markets and governments well outside the Middle East, the immediate concern is energy security. A prolonged closure or disruption of Hormuz traffic would ripple through fuel prices, shipping insurance costs and inflation expectations worldwide, complicating central banks' efforts to manage interest rates. Governments dependent on Gulf oil imports are likely to watch the coming days closely for signs of whether this marks a contained exchange or the start of a broader and more prolonged confrontation.
