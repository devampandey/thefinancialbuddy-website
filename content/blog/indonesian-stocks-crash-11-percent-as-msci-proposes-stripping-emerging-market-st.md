Indonesia's stock market suffered its heaviest one-day fall in nearly three decades on Friday after index provider MSCI proposed removing the country from its emerging-market universe altogether.

The IDX Composite opened 8% lower at about 5,394, down from Thursday's close of 5,863.78, which automatically triggered a 30-minute trading halt on the Indonesia Stock Exchange. Selling resumed as soon as the halt ended, and the index slid as much as 11.4% intraday. At around 5,240 it was down about 614 points, or 10.6%, from the previous close. It was the steepest single-session drop since January 1998, at the height of the Asian financial crisis, and it leaves the benchmark down close to 40% for the year.

## What MSCI is proposing

Late on Thursday, MSCI opened a consultation on reclassifying the MSCI Indonesia indexes from Emerging Market to Standalone Market. Notably, it rejected the softer option of a downgrade to Frontier Market status, saying feedback from investors suggested that move would not properly capture the depth of the problems.

The index provider said global investors had flagged persistent opacity in shareholding structures and suspected coordinated trading as far back as its June 2026 market classification review. Jakarta has since announced reforms, including disclosure of shareholders owning more than 1%, more detailed investor classification data, reporting on high shareholding concentration and a roadmap to a 15% minimum free float. MSCI said institutional investors still wanted to see consistent implementation and evidence of meaningful enforcement before those steps could change the picture.

MSCI said it expects to go ahead with the reclassification unless the consultation turns up clear evidence of material improvement. Feedback will be collected until April 30, 2027, with a final decision due on or before June 30, 2027. In the meantime, it will keep its existing freeze on Indonesian securities, meaning no increases to foreign inclusion factors or share counts and no new additions to its Indonesia investable market index.

## Why the reaction was so violent

A standalone market sits outside MSCI's flagship emerging-market benchmarks, which are tracked by trillions of dollars in passive and active funds. Exit from those indexes would force tracker funds to sell Indonesian holdings, and analysts have put potential passive outflows at around $3 billion. That comes on top of heavy foreign selling already this year.

The pressure spilled into the currency. The rupiah weakened to about 18,070 per US dollar, its softest level since early August and close to its record low near 18,190.

The Indonesia Stock Exchange said it would ask MSCI to explain the rationale and criteria behind the proposal. Its president director, Jeffrey Hendrik, stressed that Thursday's announcement marks the start of a consultation rather than a final verdict, and pointed to the reforms already under way. Rival index provider FTSE Russell has so far kept Indonesia in its emerging-market category.

## What it means beyond Jakarta

For other emerging markets, including India, the episode is a reminder that index providers increasingly treat market governance, free float and ownership transparency as conditions of membership, not just market size or liquidity. If Indonesia is eventually removed, its weight in emerging-market benchmarks would be redistributed among the remaining members, a modest mechanical tailwind for larger constituents.

The more immediate question is whether Indonesian authorities can show enough enforcement progress over the next eight months to change MSCI's mind. Friday's sell-off suggests investors are not betting on it yet.
