The International Monetary Fund has raised its growth forecast for India to 7% for 2026, an upgrade of 0.6 percentage point from the projection it published in July. The revision, part of the Fund's October World Economic Outlook released on Tuesday, keeps India among the fastest-growing large economies at a time when the global picture is darkening under a prolonged energy shock.

The IMF has also held its 2027 forecast for India at 7%. On a calendar-year basis, the numbers are slightly higher: 7.4% for 2026 and 7% for 2027. India grew 7.8% in 2025, so the new projection still implies a modest slowdown, just a much milder one than the Fund expected three months ago.

## Why the Fund Turned More Optimistic

The IMF pointed to two engines: resilient domestic demand and strong services activity. Both have held up even as crude prices climbed above $100 a barrel and India's retail inflation rose to a 23-month high of 5.54% in September. Investment linked to artificial intelligence was also cited as a supporting factor.

The upgrade lands at an awkward moment for Indian policymakers. The Reserve Bank of India raised its repo rate earlier this month for the first time since 2023, and food inflation has run hot. A stronger growth outlook gives the central bank more room to focus on prices without worrying as much about choking activity.

## The Global Picture Is Far Weaker

The report, titled "Resilience Under Strain, Urgent Choices," paints a subdued picture for the world as a whole. The IMF expects global output to grow 3.1% in 2026, slightly better than the 3% it projected in July but well below the 3.5% pace of 2025. It sees a recovery to 3.4% in 2027.

Emerging and developing economies as a group are forecast to slow sharply, from 4.5% in 2025 to 3.7% in 2026, before rebounding to 4.4% in 2027. China's growth is projected to ease to 4.5% this year, weighed down by expensive oil, uncertainty and long-running structural problems.

The Fund said headline global numbers hide wide differences between countries. Two forces are increasingly sorting winners from losers: how exposed an economy is to the war-driven energy shock, and how much it benefits from demand tied to the global technology cycle.

## The Hormuz Assumption

The forecasts rest on a key assumption about the conflict in West Asia. The IMF's baseline assumes the Strait of Hormuz reopens in March 2027, with conditions broadly returning to prewar levels by October 2027. Even then, the Fund expects energy prices to stay above where they were before the war.

It also warned that some emerging and developing economies without adequate fuel inventories could face outright shortages as buyers compete for limited cargoes. For India, a large oil importer, that is the main risk to the upgraded outlook. The IMF stressed that its higher number does not mean India is insulated from the energy shock; the impact on any country depends on whether it imports or exports energy and how directly it is exposed to the conflict.

## What It Means for Indian Households

A 7% growth forecast is good news for jobs and incomes on paper, but households are feeling the oil shock through fuel, transport and food costs. With inflation rising and the RBI now in tightening mode, borrowing costs are more likely to rise than fall in the months ahead.
