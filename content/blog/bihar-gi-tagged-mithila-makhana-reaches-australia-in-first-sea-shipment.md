Bihar's geographically-tagged Mithila Makhana has landed in Australia for the first time, with Commerce and Industry Minister Piyush Goyal announcing on Saturday that an 18-tonne consignment had been shipped from the state via sea route, marking the latest step in India's push to turn a traditional regional crop into a global export commodity.

The shipment was procured directly from farmers in Darbhanga, the heart of India's makhana-growing belt, and facilitated by the Agricultural and Processed Food Products Export Development Authority (APEDA). According to the minister, farmers linked to this consignment earned close to 18 percent more than prevailing market prices, a margin that officials are holding up as evidence that direct export linkages can meaningfully improve returns for small and mid-sized growers.

## Building on a young export corridor

The Australia shipment follows a similar first-of-its-kind consignment last month, when seven tonnes of flavoured Makhana were sent from Darbhanga to Canada by sea, also through APEDA's support. That earlier shipment reportedly fetched farmers prices more than 50 percent above what they would otherwise have received, underscoring how nascent and potentially lucrative these new international routes are for a crop that, until recently, was sold almost entirely through domestic wholesale and retail channels.

Taken together, the Canada and Australia shipments suggest a deliberate strategy: use the GI tag secured for Mithila Makhana, which legally ties the product's identity and quality reputation to the Mithila region spanning parts of Bihar, to open premium export markets rather than compete purely on volume in commodity trade.

## Why the GI tag matters commercially

A Geographical Indication tag is more than a branding exercise. It gives Mithila Makhana legal protection against imitation products trying to trade on its name and reputation, similar to how Champagne or Darjeeling tea are protected internationally. For an export-oriented crop, that protection is what allows Indian exporters and importing retailers abroad to market the product as an authentic, traceable specialty item rather than a generic commodity snack, supporting higher price points in health-food and gourmet retail segments overseas.

Makhana, also known as fox nuts or lotus seeds, has seen rising global demand as a gluten-free, protein-rich snack, a positioning that aligns well with health-conscious consumer trends in markets like Australia, Canada, the United States and parts of Europe. Bihar accounts for the overwhelming majority of India's makhana production, making the state the primary beneficiary of any export growth in the category.

## What it means for farmers and policy

For now, the volumes involved, 18 tonnes to Australia and seven tonnes to Canada, are modest in the context of Bihar's overall makhana output. But the government's messaging around these shipments points to an intent to scale: officials have framed both consignments as pilot demonstrations meant to prove the commercial case for direct sea-route exports, encourage further investment in processing and value-addition facilities, and eventually formalise more regular trade corridors.

If the pricing premiums farmers are reportedly receiving hold up as volumes increase, the makhana export push could become a template for how India tries to commercialise other GI-tagged regional agricultural products, moving them from local mandis into higher-value international retail and specialty food shelves.
