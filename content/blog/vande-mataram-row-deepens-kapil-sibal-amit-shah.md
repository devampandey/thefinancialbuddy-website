A political row over how much of "Vande Mataram" should be sung at public events sharpened on Friday, with Rajya Sabha MP Kapil Sibal firing back at Union Home Minister Amit Shah's charge that the Congress party's stance was "anti-national."

## What Triggered the Dispute

The flashpoint was a decision taken earlier this week by the Congress Working Committee (CWC) to sing only the first two stanzas of the national song at party functions, a practice the party says traces back to a 1937 resolution. The CWC's move came in the backdrop of the song's 150th anniversary and follows days of friction after the BJP accused Congress president Mallikarjun Kharge and parliamentary party chairperson Sonia Gandhi of talking among themselves while the song was played during Independence Day celebrations on August 15, prompting the BJP to demand a public apology.

## Shah's Charge

Amit Shah used the CWC's decision to renew a long-standing BJP critique of Congress history, arguing that truncating the song echoes what he described as the party's 1937 choice to split "Vande Mataram" for what he called Muslim appeasement. Shah linked that historical episode to the eventual rise of the two-nation theory and the country's partition, and said the current Congress leadership was repeating that "extreme policy of appeasement" by declining to sing the song in full, even as the government has moved to formalise singing the complete version at official functions during the anniversary year.

## Sibal's Counter

Responding on social media, Sibal did not dispute the historical claim directly but instead challenged the BJP's own record, asking whether the party and its leaders had themselves sung every stanza of the song at private and official events since 1950. He specifically named Narendra Modi's years as Gujarat chief minister and Atal Bihari Vajpayee's tenure as prime minister, asking pointedly whether the BJP would call itself "anti-national" if it had not always sung the full version. Sibal framed his questions as evidence that the BJP was practising its own "vote-bank politics" while accusing Congress of the same.

## Why It Matters

The exchange is the latest flashpoint in a broader pattern of symbolic disputes between the two parties over national identity and history, playing out just days after the country's Independence Day observances. Such rows tend to dominate news cycles and provide talking points for both sides ahead of state and byelection campaigns, even though they rarely translate into policy change. For Congress, the episode adds pressure to explain a decision rooted in its own institutional history at a moment when the BJP has sought to position full-throated nationalism as a dividing line between the parties. For the BJP, keeping the issue alive helps reinforce a narrative it has used in previous election cycles.

Neither party has signalled any move to de-escalate the exchange, and with Parliament sessions and state polls on the horizon, the row over two stanzas versus the full song appears likely to resurface in the days ahead.
