Sweden opened its polling stations on Sunday for a general election that pollsters and political analysts describe as one of the tightest in the country's modern history, with the outcome likely to determine whether a far-right party enters government in Stockholm for the first time.

Voting began in the morning and runs until 8 PM local time, after which the Election Authority is expected to release preliminary figures later in the evening. Around 8 million people are eligible to cast ballots in a nation of roughly 10.6 million, and turnout has historically topped 80%, underscoring how closely contested this vote is being watched both at home and abroad.

## Two Blocs, One Dead Heat

The contest pits incumbent Prime Minister Ulf Kristersson's four-party centre-right alliance against a left-leaning bloc led by Magdalena Andersson, head of the Social Democrats. For much of the campaign, opinion polls had given Andersson's coalition a comfortable edge, but that lead narrowed sharply in the final days, with several trackers now showing the two sides separated by fractions of a percentage point.

What has drawn particular attention is the position of the Sweden Democrats, a party with roots that trace back to neo-Nazi fringe movements in the late 1980s. Recent polling put the party at close to 19% support, making it the second-largest force in the race behind the Social Democrats. Kristersson's alliance has indicated it would lean on Sweden Democrat backing to secure a second term, a prospect that would mark the party's first formal role at the heart of a Swedish government.

Andersson has framed the choice starkly for voters, asking whether the country wants "a government that will be totally dominated by the Sweden Democrats" or one led by her own party. Her opponents counter that a left-led coalition would struggle to agree on economic policy, immigration rules, and energy strategy given the ideological range of the smaller parties propping it up.

## What's At Stake

Beyond the composition of the next government, the election is being read as a broader test of how far-right movements across Europe can convert sustained polling gains into actual governing power. Sweden has historically maintained a "cordon sanitaire" against parties with extremist origins, and a Sweden Democrat foothold in the coalition would represent a significant break from that tradition.

Economic policy, immigration and integration, energy security, and Sweden's continuing role within NATO and the European Union have all featured prominently in campaign debates. Both blocs have also had to contend with a slowing economy and public unease over rising living costs, themes that have echoed across several European electorates this year.

Exit polls are expected shortly after voting closes, though Swedish election authorities have cautioned that a result this close may take longer than usual to confirm, particularly if postal and overseas ballots prove decisive in marginal constituencies.

The vote is being closely watched internationally, including by investors tracking the euro-area's political stability and by governments across the Nordic region assessing what a change in Stockholm could mean for regional defence and energy cooperation.
