The United States on Friday imposed sanctions on the International Criminal Court as an institution, a sharp escalation of the Trump administration's campaign against the Hague-based tribunal. Until now Washington had targeted individual judges, prosecutors and people who helped the court. This time it named the court itself, which could cut it off from the American companies that provide its banking, software and other services.

Secretary of State Marco Rubio announced the measures, calling the ICC a rogue court and saying the US would ban transactions with it. He framed it as a choice for the court: either the ICC ends what Washington calls its threats against Americans, or the US will end the ICC.

## What the sanctions mean in practice

US sanctions usually stop all American firms from doing business with the target. Banks close accounts, insurers end cover and technology providers withdraw support. For a court with about 900 staff, that is a serious operational threat. ICC officials had reportedly spent months preparing for this, moving office computers from Microsoft software to open-source alternatives and arranging new banking and health insurance.

Rubio said the action had been structured to give the court and its member states a chance to address US concerns. A senior State Department official said the Treasury would issue licences to protect American interests, which suggests some temporary exemptions. Rubio also said Washington expects allies, many of which are ICC members and rely on US forces for their defence, to rein in the court.

## Why Washington is targeting the court

The administration's complaints centre on two issues: the ICC's arrest warrants for Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu and former defence minister Yoav Gallant over the Gaza war, and the court's past inquiry into conduct in Afghanistan that could have involved US personnel. Neither the US nor Israel is a party to the Rome Statute, the treaty that created the court. The ICC's position is that crimes committed on the territory of member countries fall within its jurisdiction regardless of the suspect's nationality. It has not prosecuted any US citizen.

## A collision course with Europe

The timing was striking. The sanctions came hours after the Nobel Peace Prize went to South African jurist Navi Pillay, who sat on the ICC's Appeals Chamber from 2003 to 2008 ([see our earlier coverage](/world/navi-pillay-south-african-jurist-of-indian-tamil-origin-wins-2026-nobel-peace-pr)). The Nobel committee's citation warned that judges and institutions of international law are under political attack.

The court has 125 member states, including every EU country and every NATO member except the US and Turkey. Earlier this week the ICC's registrar urged the EU to be ready to use its blocking statute, a legal tool meant to shield European entities from the reach of foreign sanctions. How Brussels responds will be the key test of whether European backing for the court amounts to more than statements.

## Reaction

The ICC rejected the sanctions as an attack on the rule of law and the international legal order. Deputy prosecutor Nazhat Shameem Khan said the measures must be condemned and that the court's investigations and prosecutions would continue. UN Secretary-General António Guterres called the move a serious blow.

The pressure comes as the court's membership shows strain. Chad and Venezuela moved to withdraw this year, following Burkina Faso, Mali and Niger, and some members have long complained that international justice falls hardest on countries in the Global South.

## What to watch

Watch for the Treasury's licences and the exemptions they carry, any EU decision to activate its blocking statute, and whether banks and software suppliers outside the US also cut ties with the court because of the risk of secondary penalties.
