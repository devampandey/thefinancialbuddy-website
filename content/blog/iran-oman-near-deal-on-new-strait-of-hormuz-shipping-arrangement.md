Iran and Oman say they are on the verge of finalising a new arrangement governing shipping traffic through the Strait of Hormuz, one of the world's most important oil transit corridors, in what would mark a significant step toward de-escalating tensions tied to the broader US-Iran standoff. Officials from both countries describe a joint statement as being in its final drafting stage, though key details are still being worked out.

## What the Emerging Deal Involves

Under the framework described by Iranian officials, Iran would oversee vessels entering the Strait of Hormuz while Oman would manage ships departing through it, effectively splitting responsibility for monitoring the narrow waterway between the two countries. Tehran has said the two sides have already agreed on the specific coordinates for the shipping routes involved, suggesting the technical groundwork for an arrangement is largely complete even as the political and financial terms remain unsettled.

## The Sticking Points

The most contentious issue is money. Iran is reportedly seeking a fee equivalent to 5% to 7% of the value of cargo passing through the strait under the new arrangement, while Oman has proposed a lower figure of around 3%. That gap has slowed final agreement even as both sides describe the broader deal as close.

Washington has pushed back on characterisations of the emerging arrangement, rejecting suggestions that it would hand Iran effective control over traffic through the strait. US officials have argued that any temporary shipping route should operate without requiring permits, approvals or fees from Iran, putting the American position at odds with the fee structure Tehran is pursuing. This disagreement highlights that even if Iran and Oman reach bilateral terms, the arrangement's acceptance by other stakeholders, including shipping companies and Western governments, is not guaranteed.

## Why the Strait of Hormuz Matters

The Strait of Hormuz is one of the busiest and most strategically sensitive oil chokepoints globally, with a substantial share of the world's seaborne crude oil and liquefied natural gas passing through it daily. Disruptions or uncertainty around the strait tend to ripple quickly through global energy prices, and any credible move toward stabilising navigation there is closely watched by oil-importing economies. It is worth noting that the current discussions concern a specific new shipping-route mechanism rather than a full reopening or resolution of the underlying tensions in the region; the strait has not been fully closed, but the broader conflict dynamics between the US, Israel and Iran continue to create periodic uncertainty for shippers and insurers.

## What to Watch Next

The coming days will likely determine whether Iran and Oman can bridge the remaining gap on fees and finalise the joint statement both sides say is nearly ready. Equally important will be how Washington and international shipping interests respond once the terms are made public, since their acceptance—or rejection—of the arrangement will shape whether it meaningfully reduces uncertainty in the region or becomes another unresolved flashpoint. Given how closely oil markets track developments in the strait, any formal announcement is likely to move energy prices in the short term.
