A quiet but significant shift has been underway in Indian equity markets, one that has little to do with any single day's headline. Since the market's September 2024 peak, foreign institutional investors (FIIs) have withdrawn close to $58 billion from Indian stocks over roughly 22 months, a sustained pullback that has steadily eroded their footprint in the country's largest companies. Domestic institutional investors (DIIs), meanwhile, have more than filled the void.

## Ownership Tables Have Flipped

Data on Nifty 500 constituents shows FII ownership has fallen to around 17%, its lowest level in recent memory. DII ownership, by contrast, has climbed to a record 21%, marking the ninth straight quarter of gains. That crossover is a structural change from the pattern that defined Indian markets for much of the past decade, when foreign flows typically set the tone for major indices.

On a single-day basis, the picture looked calmer: FIIs were actually net buyers of close to ₹2,450 crore in the cash segment on August 4, a reminder that the longer-term outflow trend does not preclude occasional buying sessions. But the broader direction over nearly two years has clearly favored domestic capital.

## What's Driving Foreign Selling

Analysts point to a familiar mix of global factors. Elevated US interest rates and a strong dollar have made emerging-market assets relatively less attractive, prompting global funds to reallocate toward US Treasuries and other dollar-denominated instruments. Broader risk aversion, tied to geopolitical uncertainty, has compounded the trend. On top of that, India's equity valuations, which ran ahead of many emerging-market peers over the past few years, have left less room for comfort as global capital turns more selective, encouraging profit-booking by foreign funds.

## Domestic Flows Provide a Cushion

The offsetting force has been the steady rise of domestic participation, powered by continued inflows into mutual funds, insurance companies, and pension funds. Record SIP contributions and growing retail participation through domestic institutions have given Indian markets a cushion that didn't exist in earlier foreign-selling episodes, when indices tended to fall sharply in the absence of matching local demand.

## Rupee Reflects the Pressure

The rupee has borne some of the strain from this capital rotation. The currency was trading near 95.2 to the US dollar, essentially flat on the day but down roughly 8.5% over the past twelve months, even as it has firmed marginally, by about 0.2%, over the past month. A weaker rupee makes imports costlier and can complicate the central bank's inflation calculus, adding another variable for policymakers to track alongside capital flows.

## What It Means Going Forward

For now, the domestic bid has kept headline indices resilient despite the scale of foreign selling, but market participants note that a reversal in FII sentiment, should US rates ease or risk appetite for emerging markets return, could still mark the next major inflection point for Indian equities. Until then, the ownership shift toward domestic capital looks likely to continue defining how Indian markets absorb global shocks.
