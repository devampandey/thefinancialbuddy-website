Tata Motors and Amazon said on Tuesday that they will deploy 3,000 electric trucks across Amazon's transportation network in India. Amazon called it the largest electric-truck rollout by any e-commerce company in the country. Heavy Industries Minister H D Kumaraswamy flagged off the first batch in Delhi, and an Amazon executive said the full fleet will be on the road within three years.

Tata Motors shares rose about 5% to around Rs 402.55 by early afternoon. The broader market was slightly lower.

## How the deal is structured

Tata Motors will build and supply the trucks. It will not own them, and neither will Amazon. Amazon's carrier partners, the independent logistics firms that move its parcels, will buy and operate the vehicles. That arrangement keeps fleet costs off Amazon's balance sheet and means Tata does not have to finance the vehicles. It also shifts the commercial risk onto smaller operators, who will need the trucks' running-cost savings to cover their higher upfront price.

The first vehicles are Tata Ultra E.7 light and medium-duty trucks. The model uses an integrated electric axle, which combines the motor and drivetrain in one unit, along with regenerative braking that recovers energy when the truck slows, fast-charging capability and high-voltage safety systems. The trucks will begin with middle-mile work, carrying shipments between warehouses and sorting centres and between cities, rather than doorstep deliveries.

## Why Delhi first, and why now

The timing follows both the market and local policy. The rollout coincides with the festive season, when parcel volumes peak. It also lines up with Delhi's 2026 electric-vehicle policy. Under that policy, the first 1,000 privately owned medium-duty electric trucks registered within three months of its launch get a 10-year permit to enter the city at any hour, free of the time-window restrictions that limit diesel goods vehicles. For a logistics operator, round-the-clock access to the capital is worth a lot in itself.

## The bigger ambition: NH48

Beyond Delhi, the two companies want to electrify the roughly 2,800-km Delhi-Bengaluru-Chennai freight corridor on National Highway 48. The route crosses seven states and links six of Amazon's largest e-commerce regions. Charging will come from Yahhvi, which has already built 10 charging yards for Amazon in India and is developing new hubs along the route.

Getting there will be the hard part. Medium-duty electric trucks run well on short, predictable urban routes where they can charge overnight at a depot. Long highway hauls need dependable fast chargers at regular intervals, and today those are rare in India. The pace at which the charging network grows along NH48 will largely determine how many of the 3,000 trucks end up on intercity routes rather than city loops.

## What it means for Tata Motors

For Tata Motors, which has been expanding its electric commercial-vehicle range, the deal provides a large, visible reference customer in a segment where scale decides the economics. Management has said electric trucks are still less profitable than diesel ones at current volumes. A steady 3,000-unit order over three years could help bring down battery and component costs and build out the service network those vehicles need. Pricing and the delivery schedule have not been disclosed, so the near-term effect on earnings is hard to estimate.

Kumaraswamy framed the deal as filling a gap in India's EV transition. Electric two-wheelers and passenger cars have gained ground, he said, but freight transport needs to electrify too as the economy grows and demand for logistics rises.
