Three of the most powerful security officials in the Muslim world arrived in Tehran on Monday on a joint mission to keep the eight-month Iran war from flaring up again. Saudi Defence Minister Prince Khalid bin Salman, Pakistan's army chief Field Marshal Asim Munir and Turkey's intelligence chief Ibrahim Kalin were due to meet Mohsen Rezaei, secretary of Iran's Supreme National Security Council, as well as President Masoud Pezeshkian.

An Iranian official confirmed the delegations had arrived. The visit was first announced by Ali Bagheri Kani, Rezaei's deputy, who told Iranian television that the idea took shape during Munir's own trip to Tehran last week.

## A new regional grouping steps in

Iranian and Saudi officials framed the meeting as the work of the so-called Mecca Alliance, a grouping that brings Riyadh, Islamabad and Ankara together on regional security. Saudi Arabia's deputy foreign minister for public diplomacy, Raid Krimly, said the visit was meant to ease tensions in the US-Iran standoff and to back mediation already being run by Qatar and Pakistan.

Tehran, for its part, used the occasion to repeat a familiar line: that the region's security should rest on its own states rather than on outside powers. Bagheri Kani said Iran opposed external intervention and influence in West Asia.

The composition of the delegation is telling. Saudi Arabia is sending its defence minister rather than a diplomat, Pakistan its army chief, and Turkey its spymaster. That suggests the talks are about concrete military de-escalation, not just statements of goodwill.

## Why now

The timing is driven by two elections. Israel votes on Tuesday, and the United States holds midterm elections on November 3. Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu warned on Sunday that Iran or its proxies might try to strike before the Israeli vote, and threatened blows far heavier than Tehran could imagine. Israeli media also reported a clash between Netanyahu and his army chief over an order the military feared could restart full-scale fighting, a report Netanyahu dismissed.

In Washington, President Donald Trump has said he is weighing a deal with Iran against military action. Iran's foreign ministry said over the weekend that it had not shut the door on diplomacy and that messages were still being passed to the US through intermediaries, while rejecting what it called American demands for complete surrender.

## Yemen is on the table too

For Riyadh, the most immediate concern is closer to home. The Houthis in Yemen, who are aligned with Iran, have stepped up missile and drone attacks on Saudi Arabia and on Saudi-backed forces inside Yemen. Early on Monday the group claimed fresh ballistic missile strikes on government forces in Aden and Lahj. Officials familiar with the Tehran talks said the Yemen war and Houthi attacks on the kingdom would be discussed.

Saudi Arabia said it hoped the visit would help find ways to de-escalate tensions and strengthen regional security.

## Why markets are watching

The stakes extend well beyond the region. The war has already disrupted tanker traffic around the Strait of Hormuz and kept oil prices volatile, a direct cost for importers such as India. Any sign that the three visitors have secured commitments from Tehran, or a channel to Washington, would ease some of that pressure. A breakdown, on the eve of Israel's election, would do the opposite.
